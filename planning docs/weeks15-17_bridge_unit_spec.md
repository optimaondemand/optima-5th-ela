# Weeks 15–17 Bridge Writing Unit — Master Spec

## Why this unit exists

Weeks 1–14 and Week 18+ are already built and must NOT be touched or renumbered. The original scope called for *Soft Rain* in Weeks 15–18, but that novel has been cut — there is no text, no OCR, and Week 18 is already built as *How I Became a Ghost* (Tim Tingle) Ch. 4–7. This unit fills Weeks 15–17 as a **standalone 3-week Writing Workshop** that bridges *Johnny Tremain* (Weeks 6–8, Revolutionary Boston) and *How I Became a Ghost* (Week 18+, the Choctaw Trail of Tears). It is NOT a chapter-based novel week — treat it as a **Writing Project Week** per SKILL.md's "Two Week Types" (Step 0): normal strands continue in modified form, but the writing process replaces the chapter-comprehension arc.

**Essential Question for the whole unit:** *How do ordinary young people experience extraordinary moments in history?*

This connects *Johnny Tremain*'s question ("What does freedom mean? How do revolutions affect ordinary people?") to *How I Became a Ghost*'s question ("What happens when another group's freedom is taken away? How do historical events look from a different perspective?"). Students move from one novel to the next through historical empathy, not just a change of book.

**CRITICAL HISTORICAL ACCURACY NOTE:** The next novel, *How I Became a Ghost* by Tim Tingle, is about the **Choctaw** Nation's removal (Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, 1830; forced march 1831–1833), narrated by a Choctaw boy named Isaac. It is NOT about the Cherokee (that was the cut *Soft Rain* novel). Week 16 research content must be about the **Choctaw Nation**, not the Cherokee. Do not mix these up.

**Verified facts for Week 16 (from Wikipedia/Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma/Oklahoma Historical Society, checked July 2026):**
- Indian Removal Act signed 1830.
- Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek: signed September 27, 1830; proclaimed February 24, 1831. First removal treaty carried out under the Indian Removal Act.
- The Choctaw ceded about 11 million acres of homeland (mostly in Mississippi) in exchange for about 15 million acres in Indian Territory (later Oklahoma).
- Removal happened in three waves, 1831–1833, including a brutal winter (1831) and a cholera epidemic (1832).
- Roughly 15,000 Choctaw people (plus about 1,000 enslaved people they held) made the journey; about 2,500 died along the way.
- The Choctaw were the first of the "Five Tribes" forcibly removed from the Southeast.
- About 5,000–6,000 Choctaw people stayed behind in Mississippi and faced increasing harassment.
- Do NOT invent quotations or dialogue attributed to real Choctaw people. Where a perspective is needed, either paraphrase documented historical positions/policy (U.S. government/settler expansion arguments) in neutral third person, or use only real, attributed reflection such as the Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma's own public statement that the treaty "forever changed our Chahta people's story" (attribute it as "the Choctaw Nation today reflects..."). Do not put invented first-person words in the mouth of a specific historical Native person.
- Do NOT preview or spoil *How I Became a Ghost*'s plot (Isaac, the ghost narrator, supernatural elements). Week 16 only builds background/context so Week 18 lands with more meaning.

# WEEK 15 — Becoming a Historical Writer

**Big idea:** How do writers create believable historical worlds? Study Johnny Tremain as a mentor text for historical-fiction craft, then practice short craft-focused pieces (not a full story yet).

**Source material:** `optima-5th-ela/planning docs/Johnny tremain novel copy/Johnny Tremain - Esther Hoskins Forbes.pdf` (real text PDF — use `pdftotext` to extract; Chapter I "Up and About" begins right after the introduction, roughly line 179 in a pdftotext dump). Pull sentences only from the actual text — do not invent Johnny Tremain quotes.

**Confirmed excerpt to use (Chapter I opening, verified from the PDF):**
> "ON ROCKY ISLANDS gulls woke. Time to be about their business. Silently they floated in on the town, but when their icy eyes sighted the first dead fish, first bits of garbage about the ships and wharves, they began to scream and quarrel."

And the Lapham household dialogue later in the same chapter:
> "'Look here, Johnny, I'm not getting up 'cause you tell me to. I'm getting up 'cause Mrs. Lapham tells me to.' 'All right,' said Johnny blandly, 'just so you're up.'"

And Johnny's introduction:
> "He was a rather skinny boy, neither large nor small for fourteen. He had a thin, sleep-flushed face, light eyes, a wry mouth, and fair, lank hair."

Extract 2-3 more short excerpts as needed from Chapters I and V ("The Boston Observer," which shows Rab's print shop — good for the "form/audience" lesson on Day 3) by running `pdftotext` on the PDF yourself and reading the relevant chapter.

**Word Study strand topics (all 4 days):**
- Grammar: Dialogue punctuation and tags (quotation marks, tag placement, comma before tag)
- Spelling: Syllable patterns in historical/domain vocabulary (apprentice, silversmith, wharf, colonial) — Day 1 uses the discovery method (Step 9)
- Morphology: Roots **scrib / script** — "to write" (describe, subscribe, manuscript, inscription) — ties directly into "becoming a writer"
- Vocabulary words (from the mentor excerpts): apprentice, wharf, silversmith, crucible, apprentice's — pick 4-6 that actually appear in the excerpts you pull

### Day 15.1 — Sensory Detail & Balancing Fact/Imagination
- Craft focus: how historical fiction blends real history with an imagined character; showing daily life through the five senses
- Mentor excerpt: the gulls/Boston-waking opening
- WGRD (Tab 1): "Good historical fiction writers notice small daily-life details before they notice big historical events. As you study today's passage, ask yourself: What senses does the author use? What ordinary task is happening? How does this make 1773 Boston feel real?"
- DOL: Sentence 1 = the gulls opening sentence (or a nearby sentence with a clear correct convention to notice); Sentence 2 = a different sentence about Boston mornings with deliberate errors
- Comp/Craft Qs (Tab 4): (1) What sensory details does Forbes use to show morning in Boston? Cite at least two. (2) Why does starting with animals and ordinary chores instead of a historical event help the reader believe in this world? (3) What does the household dialogue reveal about daily apprentice life?
- Writing Connection (the day's product): Write a historical journal entry (5–7 sentences) from the perspective of a young person living in 1773 Boston, waking up on an ordinary morning. Use at least 2 sensory details and 1 line of dialogue.

### Day 15.2 — Authentic Dialogue Reveals Character
- Craft focus: dialogue shows personality and relationships without the narrator explaining
- Mentor excerpt: the Dove/Johnny exchange
- Poetry Corner: a short public-domain poem about colonial daily life or morning routine (choose something genuinely public domain — e.g., an excerpt of a short Longfellow poem about early-morning Boston/harbor imagery) + Socratic question
- RWM: use "'All right,' said Johnny blandly, 'just so you're up.'" — Read like a Reader (what does Johnny's calm reveal about his confidence?) / Read like a Writer (notice the placement of the dialogue tag and the adverb "blandly") / Mimic (sentence frame: "'___,' said [Name] ___ly, '___.'")
- DOL: different sentence pulled from the same dialogue exchange
- Comp/Craft Qs: about what the dialogue reveals about each character, and how word choice in dialogue tags shapes tone
- Writing Connection: Write a short diary entry (5–6 sentences) that includes one line of dialogue revealing your character's personality — do not have the narrator explain the personality directly; let the dialogue do the work.

### Day 15.3 — Audience and Purpose (Choosing a Form)
- Craft focus: how form (letter, journal, newspaper article, field notebook) changes voice and which details get included
- Mentor excerpt: pull a short passage from Chapter V ("The Boston Observer") showing the print shop/newspaper world — extract it yourself from the PDF
- WGRD shifts to Tab 3 starting this day
- Comp/Craft Qs: about how the newspaper-world passage differs in voice/purpose from the Chapter I diary-like opening
- Writing Connection: Choose ONE form (newspaper article, field notebook entry, or letter) and write a short piece (6–8 sentences) about a historical event, choosing details appropriate to that form.

### Day 15.4 — Synthesis: Letter From a Historical Character
- Craft focus: reflect on and combine the week's craft moves (sensory detail + dialogue + audience awareness)
- Mentor excerpt: brief callback to the Chapter I opening and a short excerpt from the Chapter IV trial scene (contrast an "ordinary morning" with an "extraordinary moment") — extract the trial scene from the PDF
- Poetry Corner Day 4: same or a different public-domain poem + deeper Socratic question ("How does the poet's word choice create urgency or stillness? What does this have in common with the mentor texts we studied this week?")
- Comp/Craft Qs: include ONE reflective cross-week question — "What craft move from this week (sensory detail, dialogue, or choosing a form) will help you most when you write your own historical fiction next week? Why?"
- Writing Connection: Write a letter (6–8 sentences) from a historical character to a loved one, describing an extraordinary event using at least one sensory detail and one line of dialogue.

**Fluency (lesson-15-fluency.html):** Use the Chapter I gulls-waking passage or the Dove/Johnny exchange (whichever reads more expressively aloud) — 3-5 sentences, pulled verbatim from the PDF.

**Check-in (lesson-15-checkin.html):** Grammar (dialogue punctuation correction/identification), morphology (scrib/script word-part breakdown), spelling (historical vocabulary syllable sort), reading/craft (identify a craft move — sensory detail or dialogue — in a short new excerpt), writing (one RWM-style mimic sentence).

# WEEK 16 — Research + Perspective

**Big idea:** What was happening in America in the 1830s that Johnny Tremain never showed? Build historical background on the Choctaw Nation's removal so students are ready to meet Isaac in *How I Became a Ghost* — without previewing the novel's plot.

**Central research question for the week:** How might the same historical event be remembered differently by different people?

**Source facts (already verified — see top of this doc):** Choctaw homeland in Mississippi; Indian Removal Act (1830); Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek (signed Sept 27, 1830, proclaimed Feb 24, 1831); ~11 million acres ceded for ~15 million acres in Indian Territory; removal in three waves 1831–1833; brutal winter and 1832 cholera epidemic; ~15,000 traveled, ~2,500 died; first of the "Five Tribes" removed; ~5,000-6,000 remained in Mississippi and faced harassment.

**You (the lesson-builder) must write the informational passages yourself**, grounded only in the verified facts above, in a warm, respectful, age-appropriate teacher voice — plain factual narration, no invented dialogue or invented feelings attributed to real historical individuals. It's fine to describe general, well-documented daily life (farming, homes, community structures) in neutral, respectful terms.

**Word Study strand topics (all 4 days):**
- Grammar: Sentence variety in explanatory writing (short vs. long sentences, varied openings)
- Spelling: Multisyllabic decoding with roots/affixes in historical vocabulary (removal, territory, treaty, ancestral) — Day 1 discovery method
- Morphology: Roots **migr / mov / mot** — "to move" (migrate, movement, motion, remove) — reused intentionally from the original scope's Week 15 morphology plan, since it fits this content precisely
- Vocabulary words: nation, territory, removal, homeland, treaty, ancestral

### Day 16.1 — Central Idea & Relevant Details
- Content: a short informational passage (you write it) about the Choctaw Nation's homeland in Mississippi before removal — farming, community, daily life, in respectful factual terms
- Reading skill: identify the central idea and the details that support it
- WGRD (Tab 1): "Good readers of nonfiction look for the central idea before the details. As you read today, ask: What is this passage mostly about? Which details support that idea?"
- DOL: two sentences from/inspired by your own informational passage
- Comp/Craft Qs: central idea identification + 2 supporting-detail questions
- Writing Connection: Summarize the central idea of today's passage in your own words (2–3 sentences), using at least one specific detail.

### Day 16.2 — Author's Purpose & Perspective; Fact vs. Perspective
- Content: a short passage (you write it) presenting, side by side: (a) a neutral paraphrase of the U.S. government/settler position favoring westward expansion in the 1830s, and (b) the Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma's own modern reflection that the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek "forever changed our Chahta people's story" (attribute this directly and accurately — do not invent additional quotes)
- Reading skill: distinguish fact, perspective, and personal response
- Poetry Corner: a short, clearly teacher-original poem about leaving home/journeys — universal, NOT attributed to any real culture or person
- RWM: pull a sentence from your own Day 1 or Day 2 informational passage (informational RWM is fine — adapt "Read like a Reader/Writer/Mimic" to nonfiction sentence structure)
- Comp/Craft Qs: identify which parts of the passage are fact vs. perspective; explain why the same event might be described differently by different people
- Writing Connection: Explain in 2–3 sentences why the same historical event might be remembered differently by different people, using an example from today's passage.

### Day 16.3 — Claim + Evidence + Reasoning
- Content: students build toward a short claim about why understanding multiple perspectives matters when studying history, using evidence from Days 1–2
- Reading skill: track an argument by identifying claim, evidence, and reasoning
- WGRD shifts to Tab 3
- Comp/Craft Qs: analyze a short model claim/evidence/reasoning paragraph (you write a brief model)
- Writing Connection: Write a claim sentence about why perspective matters when studying history, with one piece of supporting evidence from this week's reading.

### Day 16.4 — Comparing Primary & Secondary Sources; Looking Ahead
- Content: brief, clear explanation of primary vs. secondary sources using a concrete example (e.g., the actual 1830 treaty text as a primary source vs. a modern historian's description of it as a secondary source) — end with a brief, spoiler-free forward look: "Next week, you'll begin building your own historical fiction story. And soon, you'll meet a new narrator who will help you see this history from the inside."
- Poetry Corner Day 4: same or different original poem + deeper Socratic question
- Comp/Craft Qs: include ONE reflective cross-week question — "How has your understanding of this time period changed from Monday to today?"
- Writing Connection: Compare, in 2–3 sentences, what a primary source and a secondary source can each tell a historian.

**Fluency (lesson-16-fluency.html):** Use 3–5 expressive (not list-like) sentences from your own Day 1 homeland-description passage — choose the most descriptive, sensory sentences you wrote.

**Check-in (lesson-16-checkin.html):** Grammar (sentence variety), morphology (migr/mov/mot roots), spelling (historical vocabulary decoding), reading (central idea/author's purpose from a short new excerpt), writing (claim + evidence sentence).

# WEEK 17 — Historical Fiction Short Story (Plan → Draft → Revise → Publish)

**Big idea:** Students write an original historical fiction short story that applies the craft moves from Week 15 and the historical empathy from Week 16.

**Story requirements (all must be present in the final piece):** historically accurate setting, an original main character, a real historical event as the anchor, an internal conflict, an external conflict, dialogue, sensory description, historically appropriate vocabulary, at least one detail drawn from research (Week 16 or independent research the student does).

**Note on setting:** the story does NOT have to be about the Choctaw removal — that would risk students fictionalizing real trauma without guidance. Encourage students to set their story in EITHER Revolutionary Boston (extending Johnny Tremain's world) OR another well-documented historical moment/period they're curious about, applying the same research-grounded craft. If a student wants to write about westward expansion or removal specifically, encourage a focus on an invented settler or invented Choctaw family in *general, respectful, non-graphic* terms, grounded in the Week 16 facts, without depicting violence in detail — keep it age-appropriate. This is entirely the student's choice; do not require the removal topic.

**Word Study strand topics (all 4 days):**
- Grammar: Complex sentences / sentence combining for cause-and-effect (fits historical causation and continues Days 2-3's dialogue punctuation review from Week 15)
- Spelling: Suffix spelling changes in narrative verbs (-ed, -ing: drop-e, double-consonant, y-to-i rules) — Day 1 discovery method
- Morphology: Roots **narr / fic** — "to tell / to make or form" (narrate, narrator, fiction, fictional) — a natural, direct fit for a story-writing week
- Vocabulary words: setting, conflict, dialogue, historical, narrator, draft

### Day 17.1 — Plan & Draft the Opening
- Mini-lesson: choosing a historical setting + real event + planning character/conflict (a short story-map organizer built right into the page, not a separate download)
- Brief callback (not a cross-tab violation — just restate) to Chapter I's opening as a model of starting with sensory detail
- Include a short **teacher-written mentor model** opening (2–3 sentences) demonstrating the craft — label it clearly as a model, e.g., "Ms. ___'s example," not attributed to a real published author
- WGRD (Tab 1): "Good historical fiction writers plan their whole story before they draft — setting, character, conflict, and a real historical event to anchor it. As you plan today, ask: What time and place will I use? What does my character want? What stands in their way?"
- DOL: Sentence 1 = a sentence from the teacher-written mentor model opening; Sentence 2 = a different mentor-model-style sentence with deliberate dialogue-punctuation or sentence-boundary errors
- Comp/Craft Qs (about their OWN plan, not a text): (1) What historical setting and real event will anchor your story? Why did you choose it? (2) Who is your main character, and what do they want? (3) What sensory detail will open your story?
- Writing Connection: Draft the opening paragraph of your story (5–8 sentences): setting, character, one sensory detail.

### Day 17.2 — Draft: Conflict and Dialogue
- Poetry Corner: a short public-domain poem about courage or facing hardship (generic/universal) + Socratic question
- RWM: pull a sentence from the Day 1 teacher-written mentor model, tied to the week's complex-sentence grammar skill
- Comp/Craft Qs (about their own draft): What is the external conflict (something outside your character) in your story? What is the internal conflict (something inside your character)? What will your dialogue reveal about your character?
- Writing Connection: Draft the next section of your story (6–10 sentences): build the conflict and include at least one line of dialogue.

### Day 17.3 — Revise for Ideas, Organization, and Voice
- Focus: one guided self-revision pass covering ideas, organization, and voice together (compressed from a 5-day revision cycle into one day since this unit only has 4 days)
- WGRD shifts to Tab 3
- Comp/Craft Qs (self-assessment of their own draft): What part of your draft is strongest, and why? Where could you add a stronger sensory detail or a more precise verb? Does your dialogue sound like something your character would actually say?
- Writing Connection: Revise one paragraph of your draft — rewrite it here with at least one improvement to sensory detail, dialogue, or voice.

### Day 17.4 — Edit (CUPS) and Publish
- Focus: Capitalization, Usage, Punctuation, Spelling editing pass, then compile the final story
- Build this page after the pattern of the existing `lesson-cmb-writing-workshop.html` in this repo: a compile-and-publish workspace with labeled sections (Opening, Conflict/Dialogue, Revised Section, plus a final full-story textarea), a "Compile My Story" button, Copy to Clipboard, Download as PDF, and a turn-in checklist with confetti on completion. This can be either the Tab 4 content of `lesson-17-4-story-publish.html` or a dedicated compile page in that same file — keep it as ONE file (`lesson-17-4-story-publish.html`), not a separate document.
- Poetry Corner Day 4: deeper Socratic question connecting the poem to their own finished story
- Comp/Craft Qs: include ONE reflective cross-week/cross-unit question — "Looking back across this whole 3-week unit, how has your understanding of 'whose story gets told' changed?"
- Writing Connection / Publish: the complete, edited final story (a generously sized textarea, or the compiled workspace described above)

**Fluency (lesson-17-fluency.html):** Use a different, more emotional short excerpt from Johnny Tremain (e.g., the Chapter IV moment after the trial — "She bent and kissed his burned hand...") — extract it from the PDF — framed as "one last chance to hear this story's voice before we meet a new narrator next week."

**Check-in (lesson-17-checkin.html):** Grammar (complex sentences/cause-effect), morphology (narr/fic roots), spelling (-ed/-ing suffix rules), reading/craft (identify a craft move in a short new excerpt), writing (revise a sentence for stronger voice or sensory detail).
