What Good Readers Do
Synthesize: how does science travel?

Chapter 22 takes the story to Sumatra — literally the other side of the world from Salem. The question it answers is: do Nat’s methods work everywhere? The answer Latham gives is quiet but definitive. As you read, notice how the chapter extends the novel’s central claim (that mathematics is universal) to a geographical extreme.

🧠 Watch for

How does the Sumatra voyage test Nat’s navigation methods in a way that Salem voyages could not? What does passing this test prove?

Science is in the chapter title. How does Latham use Chapter 22 to argue that navigation is a science, not an art?

Poetry Corner
A poem about finding the infinite in the particular

Chapter 22 takes Nat’s mathematical methods to Sumatra — the other side of the world. William Blake wrote this famous opening in 1803, the same era, about seeing the universe in a grain of sand. It connects to the way Nat sees every celestial observation: a small measurement that contains the whole cosmos.

📜 Poetry Corner
from “Auguries of Innocence”
William Blake, 1803 — public domain
To see a World in a Grain of Sand
And a Heaven in a Wild Flower,
Hold Infinity in the palm of your hand
And Eternity in an hour.
💡

Blake uses paradox — a seemingly impossible statement that contains truth. Infinity in a palm? Eternity in an hour? For Nat, a lunar angle of 61 degrees and 42 minutes contains the ship’s exact position anywhere on earth. He holds the world in a number. That is the same paradox Blake is describing.

🤔 Think — Write in Your Journal

Blake says “hold Infinity in the palm of your hand.” How does Nat’s navigation work actually do something like this? What is the “grain of sand” in his work, and what is the “world” it contains?

🔤 Spelling: Apply Patterns
Cumulative review — apply all four patterns
Which word uses the assimilation rule correctly? (sub- before -port)
Correct! sub + port → support (b → p before p).
Which is the correct schwa spelling for the word meaning “one who finds position at sea”?
Correct! Navigator — the schwa in -or is the standard suffix for “one who does.”
Which suffix correctly turns “hazard” into an adjective meaning “full of hazard”?
Correct! Hazardous — use -ous after a consonant (not -ious or -eous).

Read: Chapter 22 — “Science and Sumatra”
Mathematics works on the other side of the world

Chapter 22 is a proof. The question the novel has been building toward since Chapter 1 is: is Nat’s mathematics truly reliable, or just lucky? Sumatra is the answer — distant, unfamiliar waters, no local knowledge, and Nat’s tables work perfectly. As you read, notice how Latham structures this chapter as a kind of scientific confirmation.

⏸️ Pause: The Test

What specific navigation challenge does Chapter 22 pose for Nat? What does the successful outcome prove that earlier chapters could not?

⏸️ Pause: Science in the Title

Why does Latham title this chapter “Science and Sumatra” — not just “Sumatra”? What does putting “science” in the title tell you about what Latham wants the reader to take away from this chapter?

👀 Watch How — Alex finishes the essay

Alex reads the conclusion draft and finds the "why it matters" sentence is still too vague.

BEFORE
Alex's vague ending: He showed that knowledge is important for everyone.
AFTER
Alex's concrete revision: He showed that the purpose of knowledge is not to impress but to protect — and that a person who truly understands something cannot rest until every sailor who needs it has it too.

Notice: Alex's revision is concrete: it names the PURPOSE of knowledge and describes what a person who truly understands something DOES. It doesn't just say knowledge is important — it says what Nat did with his.
