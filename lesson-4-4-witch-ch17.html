🌱 Morphology: Suffixes -able / -ible
Making adjectives that mean “capable of”

This week you learned that the suffixes -able and -ible turn verbs into adjectives meaning “capable of” or “worthy of.”

📌 Key Concept: -able / -ible Examples

bear → bearable (capable of being borne)

comfort → comfortable (capable of giving comfort)

remark → remarkable (worthy of remark)

resist → irresistible (not capable of being resisted)

✏️ Grammar: Verb Tense Consistency
Explain the purpose
📖

Remember the Rule: When writing dialogue, put quotation marks around the speaker's exact words. Use a comma to separate the dialogue tag (she said) from the quote. Start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes. End punctuation goes inside the closing quotation mark.

All week you have practiced keeping verb tenses consistent. Good writers stay in one tense unless there is a clear reason to shift — this keeps the reader grounded in the story’s timeline.

👉 From the novel

Consistent: Kit recovered quickly. She got out of bed and helped Rachel care for the others.

Inconsistent: Kit recovered quickly. She gets out of bed and is helping Rachel care for the others.

The consistent version stays in past tense throughout. The inconsistent version jumps between past and present, confusing the reader about when events happen.

🎯

Practice: Pick the Consistent Version! Tap the sentence that keeps its verb tense consistent.

The fever struck without warning and spreads through the household. The fever struck without warning and spread through the household.
Both verbs need to be past tense. “Struck” is past tense, so “spread” (also past tense) keeps the sentence consistent. “Spreads” is present tense and clashes.
Rachel begged Matthew to call the doctor, but he refused. Rachel begged Matthew to call the doctor, but he refuses.
“Begged” is past tense, so the second verb must also be past tense: “refused.” Switching to “refuses” (present) breaks the consistency.
Kit recovered quickly because she has strength from her years in Barbados. Kit recovered quickly because she had strength from her years in Barbados.
“Recovered” is past tense, so the reason clause needs past tense too: “had.” Using “has” (present) makes the timeline confusing.
🧠 Reflect

Why does consistent verb tense matter when telling a story? What happens to the reader when tenses shift without reason?

4
🔠 Spelling: Accent & Syllable Stress
Challenge — decode the stress pattern
📖

The Rule: The suffixes -ion, -tion, and -sion turn verbs into nouns meaning "the act or result of." Compare: create → creation, discuss → discussion, decide → decision. The base word often changes spelling: drop a silent e (create → creation), change a d to s (decide → decision), or double a consonant (admit → admission).

Words have syllables that are stressed (spoken louder and longer) and unstressed (spoken quickly). Knowing which syllable gets the accent helps you spell and pronounce words correctly. Try each challenge below.

de·LIR·i·ous  or  DEL·ir·i·ous?
Kit was delirious with fever. Which syllable gets the stress?
de-LIR-i-ous — the stress falls on the second syllable. The “lir” is spoken loudest.
vi·TAL·i·ty  or  VIT·al·i·ty?
Kit’s vitality from Barbados helped her recover. Where is the accent?
vi-TAL-i-ty — the stress falls on the second syllable. Say it: vi-TAL-i-ty.
MAL·a·dy  or  ma·LA·dy?
A malady swept through Wethersfield. Which syllable is stressed?
MAL-a-dy — the stress falls on the first syllable. The “mal” is spoken loudest.
con·VULSED  or  CON·vulsed?
Mercy convulsed with fever. Where does the accent land?
con-VULSED — the stress falls on the second syllable. The “vulsed” is spoken loudest.

What Good Readers Do
Reflect on the week’s reading

As you finish this week’s reading, think about what Chapter 17 reveals about the cost of principles. Matthew refuses to call Dr. Bulkeley — the one man who might save Mercy — because of a political disagreement. Rachel watches her daughter suffer and begs her husband to bend. When do principles matter more than love? When does holding firm become cruelty?

🔍 How to Do It
  1. Identify the narrator's view: Ask what the narrator knows or reveals that the characters may not — what information does the reader get that shapes how we see events?
  2. Notice character perspectives: Pay attention to how different characters interpret the same event — their beliefs, fears, and backgrounds shape what they see.
  3. Compare what's known: Ask how the narrator's information and each character's perspective create tension, irony, or sympathy in the story.
🎯

Whole-book thinking: Across Chapters 14–17, how has the author used different characters' perspectives to build tension around Kit? Where does the reader know something that the townspeople do not, and how does that gap create suspense or sympathy?
