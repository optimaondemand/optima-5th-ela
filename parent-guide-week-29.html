This Week at a Glance

Jack and Praiseworthy settle into the diggings: red shirts, flea traps, and prices that are hard to believe. At an auction, one misheard word leaves Jack owing money for a whole bushel of neckties, and he pays rather than go back on his word. Then the partners buy a burro named Stubb, say goodbye to their friends in Hangtown, and go prospecting in the mountains, where Jack's hunting trip turns into an adventure. On Day 4 your student studies how Fleischman's voice works, and revises a paragraph of their own adventure story.

💛 A note for families: words from 1963 and real history
This book was written in 1963, and three short passages this week use words from that time that are unkind and untrue. A folded note on each day's Reading tab names the words plainly and gives the true history, with no made-up voices. Chapter 13 calls some of the auction crowd Kanakas from the Sandwich Islands, an old outsider label for Native Hawaiians; in fact Native Hawaiians came to California even before the Gold Rush, mined gold in it, and gave their name to places like Kanaka Creek. Chapter 14 lists savages beside rabbits and outlaws; the hills the partners walk into were the homelands of the Nisenan, Miwok and Maidu peoples. Chapter 15 has a prospector call Native people Digger Indians and compare them to children. Many Native Californians mined gold themselves (the chapter shows women panning in baskets), and then miners took their land and rivers; California's Native population fell from about 150,000 in 1848 to about 30,000 by 1870. The same chapter mentions Chinese miners working old claims, which was common, and in 1852 California passed a $3-a-month tax aimed at them. A separate card on Day 1 explains how miners really dug for gold. Sources: Oakland Museum of California; PBS American Experience; Calisphere (University of California); California State Parks.
Reading skill
How an author's choices create voice and tone. Your student notices word choices, short and long sentences, and how calm the narrator stays even in danger.
Big idea
Integrity: keeping your word. Jack pays for the neckties; Praiseworthy says of a promised fight, “I gave my word, didn't I?”

Endings

New This Week

⛏️ Climb Out of the Coyote Hole
The week’s word game. A coyote hole is a deep shaft miners dug down to bedrock. Each clue has three choices; a right word hauls the bucket of pay dirt up one notch, and a wrong one lets it slide back down.
🌿 Virtue Watch: integrity
After reading, your student sorts everyday examples of keeping your word, picks a moment from Chapter 14, rates the road agent who gives back a stolen coat, and on Day 4 picks the moment of most integrity in the week.

Your Student’s Writing: Revising an Adventure

Writer’s Workshop
Day 1 · PlanDay 2 · DraftDay 3 · ReviseDay 4 · Submit

This week your student goes back to their own adventure story and makes one paragraph sound alive. Day 1: pick a paragraph and list three spots to change: the voice, a line of dialogue, and a vague detail. Day 2: rewrite it, starting with the dialogue, with sentence frames for help. Day 3: check it against a five-point checklist. Day 4: submit the final paragraph with one sentence about the biggest change.

🪶

Poetry Corner: Every Part at Work

Whenever the moon and stars are set,
Whenever the wind is high,
All night long in the dark and wet,
A man goes riding by.Robert Louis Stevenson, “Windy Nights” (1885)

On Day 2 your student names every part working in this galloping poem: its form, rhyme, beat, line breaks and comparisons. On Day 4 they read Carl Sandburg’s quiet six-line “Fog” (1916) and explain how the same five parts can make the opposite feeling.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. How did Jack end up with a whole bushel of neckties?Listen for: he said “ate” to a friend and the auctioneer heard “eight”; Jack paid rather than go back on his word.
  2. What was Praiseworthy’s plan for the neckties?Listen for: he knew the miners would want them soon, and the money would buy them a burro.
  3. What is a coyote hole, and what happened at one?Listen for: a deep shaft dug to bedrock; Jack fell into one and fired his gun to call for help.
  4. What happened when Praiseworthy opened the coat?Listen for: there was no map, and he went right on to the next plan: “Put some beans on to fry.”
  5. What makes Fleischman’s writing sound like nobody else’s?Listen for: funny specific details, short sentences at big moments, a calm voice in danger, dialogue that sounds like real people.
  6. Give me a word that ends in -ous, one in -ive, and one in -ity.Listen for: courageous, creative, curiosity (or any real ones).

Try at Home
