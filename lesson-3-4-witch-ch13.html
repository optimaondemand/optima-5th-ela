Sentence 1 — The Correct Model

Tap words you notice are correct. Watch the verbs — every one of them holds the same tense.

Hannah watched Kit and Nat talking and smiled because she knew they were becoming friends.

Sentence 2 — Fix It

This sentence has mistakes. Rewrite it correctly in your notebook, using the model as a guide.

nat eaton tells kit that shakespeare should of gone on with the tempest story and then he laughed

Corrected:

Nat Eaton told Kit that Shakespeare should have gone on with The Tempest story, and then he laughed.

Compare with the model: the tense shifts. “Tells” is present but “laughed” is past — change “tells” to “told” so both verbs stay in the same time frame. Also capitalize Nat Eaton, Kit, and Shakespeare; write “should have” not “should of”; italicize the title The Tempest; add a period.

✏️ Grammar: Verb Tense Consistency
Explain the importance
📖

Remember the Rule: Good writers keep their verb tense consistent within a passage. If you start in past tense (walked, said), stay in past tense unless there is a clear reason to shift. Mixing tenses confuses the reader about when events happen.

All week you have held one tense. Today, explain why it matters. Tense is how a reader knows when something is happening — shift it without a reason and the reader loses their footing in the story.

👉 From the novel

Consistent past: Nat leaned against the doorframe, watched Kit turn the page, and said nothing for a long moment.

Every verb sits in past tense, so the whole scene happens in one continuous moment. If “says” replaced “said,” the reader would suddenly be standing in the present while the rest of the scene stayed behind — and the moment would break apart.

🎯

Practice: Pick the Consistent Version! Tap the sentence that keeps every verb in the same tense.

Nat says he would take her back to the islands, and Kit reached for the book. Nat said he would take her back to the islands, and Kit reached for the book.
Hannah smiled, watched the two of them argue over Shakespeare, and knew more than she said. Hannah smiled, watched the two of them argue over Shakespeare, and knows more than she said.
Kit walks home through the dark meadow, and the lightness she had felt at the cottage closed over. Kit walked home through the dark meadow, and the lightness she had felt at the cottage closed over.

🔠 Spelling: Suffix -ive Challenge
Utilize — decide what changes before you add -ive
📖

The Rule: The suffix -ive turns a verb or root into an adjective meaning "having the quality of." Compare: act → active (having the quality of acting), create → creative. Sometimes the base word changes spelling when you add -ive: drop a silent e (create → creative) or change a letter (permit → permissive).

Adding -ive is not always as simple as sticking it on the end. Sometimes a silent e drops. Sometimes the base word changes shape entirely. Try each challenge below.

protect + -ive
Hannah’s cottage is a ______ shelter for Kit. Does anything need to change first?
protective (nothing changes — the base word ends in a consonant, so just add -ive)
create + -ive
Kit is ______ in how she teaches Prudence. Watch the silent e.
creative (drop the silent e before adding the suffix — not “createive”)
perceive + -ive
Nat is ______ about Kit’s loneliness. This one changes more than one letter.
perceptive (the base shifts from perceive to percept-, then takes -ive)
suspect + -ive
The town grows ______ of Kit. Same root you studied in morphology this week.
suspective is not a word — English uses suspicious instead. Not every base word takes -ive, and a good speller checks rather than assumes.

What Good Readers Do
Synthesize the week — conflict and rising tension

All week you have tracked conflict and rising tension. Look back across Chapters 9–13 and notice the shape of it: Kit began the week failing at the dame school, and she ends it with a secret friendship, a secret student, and two young men watching her. Every one of those is something she now has to protect. That is what rising tension looks like — not louder events, but more to lose.

🔍 How to Do It
  1. Spot the problem: Identify what characters want and what stands in their way — person vs. person, person vs. society, or person vs. self.
  2. Track the tension: Notice moments where the conflict gets worse, stakes rise, or characters face harder choices.
  3. Ask "What changed?": After each scene, ask how the conflict shifted — did it escalate, stall, or take an unexpected turn?
🎯

Whole-book thinking: Looking back at Chapters 9–13, how has the conflict in Kit's life escalated? What new tensions have emerged, and how do her choices show whether she is confronting or avoiding them?
