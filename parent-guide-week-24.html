This Week at a Glance

The novel ends this week. In Chapter 28 the wolf returns, and Naomi and Joseph save the life of Leader, the man who vowed Naomi would die. Leader lets her go home. The ghost of a famous Choctaw chief honors the four young people. In Chapter 29 the families share a meal of corn, with a hard winter and the rest of the Trail still ahead. Then your student reads two real sources from the same years, part of a treaty and part of a letter by a Choctaw chief, and writes a paragraph comparing the novel with the record.

💛 A heads-up for families
Chapter 28 has one short, fierce fight: a panther kills a wolf, and there is blood. No person is hurt, and the book ends gently. The harder part this week is the real history. The Choctaw people were forced to leave their homeland in Mississippi, and many died on the journey. The lessons say this plainly and calmly, keep the made-up story separate from the real record, and close on the fact that the Choctaw Nation is still here today, in Oklahoma.
Reading skill
Comparing historical fiction with real sources. A novel can show what something felt like. A primary source, written at the time, shows what was agreed, decided or said. Your student learns to say which is which.
Big idea
Doing right, even by an enemy. When Leader asks Naomi why she saved him, she answers that it was the right thing to do.

“It was the right thing to do. It is the Choctaw way.”Naomi, Chapter 28

The vocabulary of history
Six words a historian uses with care: a claim is a statement put forward as true, evidence supports it, and reasoning explains how. A treaty is a formal agreement between nations.claim · evidence · reasoning · treaty · removal · nation
Spelling
Long words are short words with parts added. Students sort history words by their endings and roots.settlement · declaration · sovereignty · national · testimony
Grammar
Formal style and source transitions. An essay uses exact words, no slang and no contractions, and says where each fact came from.According to the treaty of 1830, … In Tingle’s novel, …

New This Week

🌽 Ears of Corn
The week’s word game. Each long word is an ear of corn. Your student taps the parts added to the front or the end (the husks) to find the base word inside: mis | treat | ment. Three words a day.
🌿 Virtue Watch: courage
After reading, your student sorts moments, keeps a log, rates a moment on a meter, and on Day 4 picks the strongest moment of courage in the week. One moment comes from real history: Harkins choosing to go with his people.
🖼️ Art from the time
Each lesson opens with a print or painting from the 1820s to 1840s, including a portrait of the real Pushmataha, painted in 1824. He was a real Choctaw chief and a general. He died six years before the novel begins, which is why he arrives in the story as a ghost.
📜 Two real sources
Day 3 puts real words from 1830 and 1831 on the page, with a plain-words version under each. The treaty gives the dates. The letter gives a Choctaw leader’s own voice: “We go forth sorrowful, knowing that wrong has been done.”

Your Student’s Writing: A Source Comparison

Writer’s Workshop
Day 1 · PlanDay 2 · DraftDay 3 · ReviseDay 4 · Submit

Your student writes one paragraph that sets the novel beside a real source. Day 1: plan a claim, one detail from the novel, one line from a source, and the reasoning. Day 2: write a full draft in formal style. Day 3: revise with a checklist: formal style, two source transitions, a claim with evidence and reasoning, and every fact tied to its source. Day 4: write the final paragraph, and tell what a story told by a ghost taught them about real history.

Every day also has a Read Like a Writer box with one of the book’s sentences, like “The Trail still lay before us.” This week the boxes model claim, evidence and reasoning.

🪶

Poetry Corner: A Poem as a Primary Source

Ye say they all have passed away,
That noble race and brave,
That their light canoes have vanished
From off the crested wave;Lydia Huntley Sigourney, “Indian Names” (1834), opening lines, Day 2. On Day 4: William Cullen Bryant, “An Indian at the Burial-Place of His Fathers” (1824).

Both poems were written in the years of removal, so each is a piece of history. Sigourney answers Americans who said Native people had “passed away.” Bryant, a white poet, imagines a Native speaker. On Day 4 your student sets his poem beside Harkins’s own letter and asks which one to trust for how Choctaw people felt. Both poets use the word of their time, “Indian.” The lessons explain that today we name each nation.

How to help: Read the Sigourney lines aloud together. Ask what she says is still here, even after people were forced away.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Why did Naomi help save Leader?Listen for: “It was the right thing to do. It is the Choctaw way.” And afterward Leader let her go home.
  2. Was Pushmataha a real person?Listen for: yes. A real Choctaw chief and general who died in 1824. In the novel he comes as a ghost.
  3. What is a primary source? Name the two you read.Listen for: something written at the time. Part of a treaty from 1830 and part of a letter by a Choctaw chief from 1831.
  4. What is one thing the novel and the real record do not agree on?Listen for: the novel’s first page says 1830. The treaty set the real removal for 1831 to 1833.
  5. What can the novel show you that the treaty cannot?Listen for: what it felt like for one family; a ten-year-old’s voice; Choctaw beliefs about the people who have died.
  6. Read me your comparison paragraph. Where is your reasoning?Listen for: a claim, a detail from the novel, a line from a source, and a sentence that explains how they fit together.

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Says who? When someone states a fact at dinner, ask, “According to what?” Let your student name the source.
  • Casual to formal: say a slangy sentence and let your student say it the way an essay would.
  • Peel a word: find a long word on a cereal box or in the news and take off its front and end parts to find the base.
