Sentence 2 — Fix It

This sentence has mistakes. Rewrite it correctly in your notebook, using the model as a guide.

the boy a talented silversmith worked in mr laphams shop on hancocks wharf in boston

Corrected:

The boy, a talented silversmith, worked in Mr. Lapham’s shop on Hancock’s Wharf in Boston.

Compare with the model: “The boy” is not a proper noun, so no capital. “Mr. Lapham’s” and “Hancock’s Wharf” and “Boston” are proper nouns—capital letters. “A talented silversmith” is an appositive renaming “the boy”—it needs commas on both sides. Add a period at the end.

🌱 Morphology: Roots dict & scrib/script
Define · dict = say/declare · scrib/script = write

Two Latin roots appear in many English words. Dict means “say” or “declare.” Scrib and script mean “write.” Knowing these roots helps you unlock the meaning of unfamiliar words.

📌 Root Breakdown

dict (say/declare): dictate, predict, verdict, contradict

scrib/script (write): inscribe, describe, manuscript, prescribe

From Chapter 1: Mr. Lapham never wrote down what was ordered—like a man who had no script. Johnny, by contrast, always kept his notebook and could dictate exactly what the patron said.

🌱

✏️ Grammar: Appositives
Define · What is an appositive and how does it work?

An appositive is a noun or noun phrase placed right next to another noun to rename or explain it. Appositives are set off by commas. They let an author pack extra information into one sentence.

👉 The Definition Pattern

NOUN, appositive phrase, REST OF SENTENCE

“Johnny Tremain, the most skilled apprentice in the shop, handled the silver with steady hands.” — The phrase between the commas renames Johnny.

“Mr. Hancock, the richest man in New England, ordered a new sugar basin.” — The phrase between the commas tells you who Mr. Hancock is.

The Comma Test: Pull the phrase out. Does the sentence still make sense? Yes → it’s an appositive.

🎯

Practice: Appositive Spotter! Tap the appositive phrase in each sentence—the words between the commas that rename the noun before them.

Mr. Lapham, the elderly master silversmith, warned Johnny about his pride.
Dove, the clumsiest apprentice, fetched the cracked crucible from the shelf.
Paul Revere, the finest craftsman in Boston, offered to buy Johnny’s time.
The sugar basin, Mr. Hancock’s special order, had to be finished by Monday.
Mrs. Lapham, a practical and determined woman, insisted the work continue on the Sabbath.

🔠 Spelling: dict & scrib/script Roots
Define · Build word families from Latin roots

Both dict and scrib/script are Latin roots that appear throughout academic vocabulary. Learning the root helps you figure out the meaning of words you have never seen before.

📌 Build the Word Families

dict (say, declare): dictate • predict • verdict • contradict • edict

scrib / script (write): inscribe • describe • prescribe • manuscript • scripture • description

Think: When you dictate, you say words. When you inscribe, you write words. The root carries the meaning.

Argument Writing — Day 2: What Are Reasons?
★ What Is a Claim? → ○ Reasons → ○ Evidence → ○ Draft → ○ Revise → ○ Publish

🎯 Today’s focus: Learn what a CLAIM is and write one about Johnny.

👀 Watch How — Alex

Alex is writing about Johnny Tremain. Alex starts by making an observation, then turns it into a claim.

Observation (summary)

“Johnny is a good silversmith who bosses around the other apprentices.”

✗ Just says what happens. No argument.

Claim (argument)

“Johnny’s pride in his talent is actually his biggest weakness.”

✓ Takes a position. Someone could disagree.

A claim is not just what happens—it’s what you think it means. A good claim takes a position that a reader could agree or disagree with.

📋 Today’s Tool: Claim vs. Summary

Ask: Could someone disagree with this? If yes, it’s a claim. If no, it’s just a summary.

“Johnny works in a silversmith’s shop.” → ✗ Summary. No argument here.

“In Chapter 1, Johnny shows that talent without humility leads to trouble.” → ✓ Claim. Someone could disagree.

Your claim starter: “In Chapter 1, [character] shows that ___.”
