This Week at a Glance

There is no new chapter this week. Your student has read five novels this year: The Witch of Blackbird Pond, Johnny Tremain, Carry On, Mr. Bowditch, How I Became a Ghost and By the Great Horn Spoon! Now they choose one theme (courage, independence or survival) and two of those novels, and write a five-paragraph essay comparing how the two books treat it. The essay is organized by categories, so both books sit side by side in each paragraph, and one paragraph uses a real voice from the time.

💛 A note for families: three real primary sources
On Day 2 your student reads three short real sources and picks one for the essay. Henry Bigler’s diary line from Sutter’s Mill on January 24, 1848, in his own spelling. Bayard Taylor, a reporter who landed in San Francisco in August 1849. And George W. Harkins, a Choctaw leader, writing in 1831 as the Choctaw people were forced to leave Mississippi, the real history behind How I Became a Ghost: “We go forth sorrowful, knowing that wrong has been done.” Students met all three earlier in the year. A short background card, clearly marked as written by Optima, gives the real time and place of each novel. Nothing on the page is a made-up “historical” source.
Reading skill
Comparing across texts. Your student sets a lens, reads real sources closely, rereads to test a claim, and finally reads their own essay the way a reviewer would.
Big idea
Wisdom. Knowing what matters and making good choices with what you know, like Nat Bowditch checking every figure because sailors’ lives depend on it.

New This Week

📚 Two Books, One Root
The week’s word game is a memory game. Your student turns over word cards to find two words with the same root, like verdict and dictionary. Each pair ties two books on a little bookshelf together with a gold thread.
🌿 Virtue Watch: wisdom
Your student sorts everyday examples of wisdom, picks the wisest moment from the year’s books, rates a clever plan of Praiseworthy’s, and names the wisest character of the year.
🗺️ Who was real?
A Day 2 card sorts made-up characters from real people: Nathaniel Bowditch, Paul Revere and the Boston patriots, the Choctaw leader Pushmataha, and James Marshall and John Sutter.
📖 Read Like a Writer: categories
Each day a short model shows how to sort a comparison into categories, so the two books stand side by side instead of one after the other.

Five paragraphs: 1. an opening with both novels and the claim; 2–3. two category paragraphs, each with evidence from both books; 4. a real voice from the chosen primary source; 5. a closing that says what the two books say together. This essay is the first part of the year-end portfolio your student finishes next week.

Poetry Corner: The Benchmark Poem

The buffaloes are gone.
And those who saw the buffaloes are gone.Carl Sandburg, “Buffalo Dusk” (1920), first two lines

This is the poem your student has been building toward all year. On Day 2 they name every element at work in it: its shape, its repetition, the missing rhyme and the one enormous middle line. On Day 4 they explain how those choices work together to make a poem about loss.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Which two books did you choose, and what idea do they share?Listen for: a theme (courage, independence or survival) and two novels.
  2. How do the two books treat that idea differently?Listen for: a real difference, with a moment from each book.
  3. What is a primary source? Which one did you pick?Listen for: made by someone who was there; Bigler, Taylor or Harkins.
  4. What would someone who disagrees with you say?Listen for: a fair other side, and an answer with evidence.
  5. Which characters this year were real people?Listen for: Nathaniel Bowditch, Paul Revere, Pushmataha, James Marshall.

What Gets Turned In

  • Each lesson: the day’s questions, the Word Study response, the Read Like a Writer box and that day’s part of the essay, copied and pasted into Canvas
  • Day 4: the final five-paragraph comparison essay
  • Fluency: a recording of one of the five passages
  • Check-In: at the end of the week

Done well looks like: a claim that names a similarity and a difference, both books in every category, real moments or exact words as evidence, and a reason for why the difference matters.
