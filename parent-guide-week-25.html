This Week at a Glance

A new book starts this week. Day 1 is real history: the California Gold Rush, the three routes west, and the people who already lived in California. Then the novel begins. It is January 1849. Twelve-year-old Jack Flagg and Praiseworthy, his family’s butler, stow away in potato barrels on a ship sailing from Boston around Cape Horn. They hope to strike gold and save Jack’s Aunt Arabella from losing the family home. A thief steals their money, so they shovel coal to pay their way, and Praiseworthy catches the thief with a pig and some coal dust.

💛 A heads-up for families
The book is a comedy, and these chapters are gentle. Day 1 also tells a harder part of the real history plainly: about 150,000 Native Californians lived there in 1848, and twelve years later only about 30,000 were left. The novel was written in 1963. In Chapter 1, Jack imagines “wild Indians” to fight off, a phrase common then and hurtful now. The unit guide says more.
Reading skill
Building background and reading an adventure’s shape. Your student learns the real history first, then tracks the story’s problem, its obstacles, and what is at stake.
Big idea
Loyalty. Jack refuses an easy job so he can stay with his partner: “We’re partners, sir.”

New This Week

⚖️ The Assayer’s Scale
The week’s word game. Each word is a gold nugget. Your student puts it on the HEAR pan (aud), the SPEAK pan (or), or the fool’s gold tray if it only looks like one. Audacious starts with aud but means bold, so it is fool’s gold. Six words a day.
🌿 Virtue Watch: loyalty
After reading, your student sorts examples, picks the most loyal moment in Chapter 1, rates Praiseworthy’s loyalty on a meter, and on Day 4 picks the most loyal moment of the week.
🖼️ Art from the time
Each lesson opens with a map, print or painting from 1849 to 1860. Day 1’s is a real map sold in New York in 1849 to people heading for the gold fields, with the routes by Panama and Cape Horn.
📖 Read Like a Writer, every day
One sentence from the book a day, chosen to show how an adventure sets up its problem and setting, like “And then Jack had overheard Banker Stites tell Aunt Arabella that her inheritance was almost gone.”

Your Student’s Writing: An Adventure Story Plan

Writer’s Workshop
Day 1 · PlanDay 2 · DraftDay 3 · ReviseDay 4 · Submit

Over six weeks your student writes an adventure story of their own. This week is the plan. Day 1: a hero, a problem, a setting, and what is at stake. Day 2: the plan in full sentences, with frames for help, plus one hard choice for the hero. Day 3: revise the plan with a checklist and add one thing that isn’t what it seems. Day 4: submit the whole plan. The draft starts next week.

Poetry Corner: Humor and Tone

They went to sea in a Sieve, they did,
In a Sieve they went to sea:
In spite of all their friends could say,
On a winter’s morn, on a stormy day,
In a Sieve they went to sea!Edward Lear, “The Jumblies” (1871), Day 2. On Day 4: Lewis Carroll, “The Walrus and the Carpenter” (1871).

Tone is how a writer feels about the subject. Lear’s travelers sound completely sure of a silly, dangerous plan, and the gap between how they sound and what the reader sees is the joke. Carroll’s Walrus sounds very friendly to the Oysters, but the eldest Oyster only winks and stays put.

How to help: Read the Lear lines aloud in a cheerful voice, then in a worried voice. Ask which one fits the poem.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Which way would you have gone to California in 1849, and why?Listen for: around Cape Horn (more than 13,000 miles, often five to eight months), across Panama (shorter, but fever in the jungle), or the 2,000-mile California Trail.
  2. Who was already living in California?Listen for: about 150,000 Native Californians in many nations; the Gold Rush took their land and many lives, and their nations are still there today.
  3. Why did Jack and Praiseworthy run away to sea?Listen for: Aunt Arabella’s money is almost gone and she may lose the house; they hope to strike gold.
  4. How did Praiseworthy catch the thief?Listen for: coal dust on the pig; every man touched it in the dark except the thief, whose finger stayed clean.
  5. What does aud mean? What does or mean? Name a word for each.Listen for: hear (audience, audio); speak (oral, orator).
  6. Tell me your adventure plan. What is at stake?Listen for: a hero, a clear problem, a setting that makes it harder, and what happens if the hero fails.
