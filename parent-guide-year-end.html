The Five Novels

WeeksThe bookWhat your student worked on
1–5The Witch of Blackbird Pond
Elizabeth George Speare. Kit Tyler leaves Barbados for a strict Puritan town in Connecticut and finds out what it costs to stand by a friend everyone else fears.		Setting, character, conflict and theme; the roots spect, script and struct.
6–8Johnny Tremain
Esther Forbes. An apprentice silversmith with a burned hand finds a new purpose in Boston as the Revolution begins.		Historical setting, perspective and argument writing; the roots rupt, cess and dict.
9–14Carry On, Mr. Bowditch
Jean Lee Latham. A boy who has to leave school teaches himself navigation at sea and finds the mistakes in the sailors’ own books.		Biography as literary nonfiction and perseverance; the roots nav, port and graph.
15–17Writer’s Workshop
Three weeks of writing: historical detail, real sources, and a historical fiction story.
18–24How I Became a Ghost
Tim Tingle. Isaac, a Choctaw boy, tells the story of his people’s forced journey west from Mississippi, and he tells it as a ghost.		First-person narrative voice, theme, and comparing a novel with real sources; the roots vis, spect and voc.
25–30By the Great Horn Spoon!
Sid Fleischman. Jack and the butler Praiseworthy sail around Cape Horn to the gold fields to save the family home, and find out what a fortune is really made of.		Humor, tone, voice and theme in an adventure; the roots aud, curr, tract, fort and val.
31–32The Reader-Writer Portfolio
A comparison essay on two of the five novels, then a letter to a future fifth grader.

What Your Student Wrote This Year

Every week
A writing task built a step at a time: plan, draft, revise, submit.
End-of-novel projects
A Ghost’s-Eye View (How I Became a Ghost) and From Boston to the Long Wharf (By the Great Horn Spoon!), each a four-page booklet.
Spring
An adventure story of their own, a literary response about a novel’s theme, and a five-paragraph comparison of two novels.
The last week
A real letter to next year’s fifth graders, read aloud with one poem from the year.
💛

How the Year Handled Hard History

Several of this year’s books touch real history that was cruel or unfair. The lessons kept the story and the record apart and said the true history plainly and calmly. How I Became a Ghost was read alongside real words from the time: part of the 1830 Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek and a letter by Choctaw chief George W. Harkins. By the Great Horn Spoon! was written in 1963, and where it uses unkind words from that time, a short note on that day’s page named them and gave the true history. No lesson put made-up words in a real person’s mouth.

Keep Reading This Summer

  • Read every day, even ten minutes. Let your student choose.
  • Ask the year’s questions about any book: Who is telling this story? What does the main character want? What is the big idea?
  • Hunt for roots on signs, boxes and screens: port, spect, aud, graph.
  • Read aloud together. Take turns with the voices.
  • Write one letter to someone far away, about a book.
🎓

Thank You

Thank you for reading with your student this year. The last page of their portfolio is a letter to someone they will never meet, about books they loved. That is what a reader does.
