🌱 Morphology: Suffixes -ive / -ative
Turning verbs and nouns into adjectives

The suffixes -ive and -ative turn verbs or nouns into adjectives — words that describe. Today, use these words in sentences connected to Chapters 11–12.

📌 Quick Reminder

create (verb) + -ive = creative (adjective) — having the ability to create

protect (verb) + -ive = protective (adjective) — tending to protect

imagine (verb) + -ative = imaginative (adjective) — full of imagination

talk (verb) + -ative = talkative (adjective) — inclined to talk a lot

✏️ Grammar: Dialogue Punctuation
Write using correct dialogue punctuation and paragraphing
📖

Remember the Rule: Good writers keep their verb tense consistent within a passage. If you start in past tense (walked, said), stay in past tense unless there is a clear reason to shift. Mixing tenses confuses the reader about when events happen.

When characters speak, their words need quotation marks, correct end punctuation inside the marks, and a new paragraph for each new speaker.

👉 From the novel

Comma before tag: “Nobody cares where I go,” she said.

New paragraph for new speaker:

“Can you teach me?” Prudence whispered.
“Of course I can,” Kit answered.

🎯

Practice: Fix the Dialogue! Each passage has punctuation mistakes. Tap the version with correct dialogue punctuation.

🔠 Spelling: Word Building — Suffix -ive
Apply — build adjectives from base words
📖

The Rule: The suffix -ive turns a verb or root into an adjective meaning "having the quality of." Compare: act → active (having the quality of acting), create → creative. Sometimes the base word changes spelling when you add -ive: drop a silent e (create → creative) or change a letter (permit → permissive).

The schwa sound is the lazy “uh” in an unstressed syllable. It can be spelled with any vowel — that’s what makes it tricky. Today, find the schwa and spell the words correctly.

📌 Quick Reminder

separate — the schwa is the second “a” (sounds like “sep-uh-rate”)

different — the schwa is the “e” (sounds like “diff-uh-rent”)

comfort — the schwa is the “o” (sounds like “cumf-urt”)

What Good Readers Do
Track how setting reveals character

As you read today, track how Kit’s secret life at Hannah’s reveals a different side of her character. At home she’s restless and resentful. At Hannah’s she’s patient, generous, and creative. What does this contrast tell us about what Kit really needs?

🔍 How to Do It
  1. Spot the problem: Identify what characters want and what stands in their way — person vs. person, person vs. society, or person vs. self.
  2. Track the tension: Notice moments where the conflict gets worse, stakes rise, or characters face harder choices.
  3. Ask "What changed?": After each scene, ask how the conflict shifted — did it escalate, stall, or take an unexpected turn?
🧠 As you read, ask yourself

What kind of person does Kit become at Hannah’s cottage — and why can’t she be that person in Wethersfield?

What risks is Kit taking by keeping this double life? What could go wrong?
