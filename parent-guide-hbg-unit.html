About the Book

How I Became a Ghost: A Choctaw Trail of Tears Story is by Tim Tingle, a storyteller and member of the Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, and it won the American Indian Youth Literature Award. Isaac is a ten-year-old Choctaw boy in Mississippi. On the first page he tells the reader that he is a ghost, and that he was not a ghost when the book began. Then he tells how it happened, as his family is forced to leave their home and walk west.

Isaac and his friends are made up, but the history around them is real. After the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek in 1830, the United States removed the Choctaw people from their homeland in Mississippi to Indian Territory, now Oklahoma, in three groups from 1831 to 1833. Nearly 15,000 Choctaws made the move, and historians estimate about 2,500 died along the way, many in bitter winter weather and a cholera epidemic in 1832. About 5,000–6,000 Choctaws stayed in Mississippi; the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians lives there today.

We read it because it lets fifth graders see a hard chapter of American history through a child’s eyes, told by a Choctaw author, with a family that loves each other, a talking dog, and real humor alongside the sorrow.

“I am a ghost. I am not a ghost when this book begins, so you have to pay very close attention.”Isaac, Chapter 1

Story and History: What’s What

Choctaw storytelling
Isaac sees ghosts and visions, his dog Jumper talks, and his friend Joseph can become a panther. These come from the Choctaw storytelling tradition Tim Tingle grew up with. Your student reads them as part of the story, the way they would a myth or legend.
The blankets
In the novel, the soldiers’ blankets carry smallpox, and Isaac’s mother keeps her family away from them. That is part of Tingle’s story. The history sources we checked record that most of the Choctaws who died on removal died of cold and disease, including cholera, and don’t describe infected blankets. The lessons keep the two apart, and Week 24 is built around comparing the novel with real history.

How a Week Works

Every week has four lessons, one fluency reading, and a Check-In. Each lesson follows the same path, so your student always knows what comes next. Across the four days, the grammar and word skills build:

Day 1Notice: work out the rule
Day 2Identify: find and sort
Day 3Use: build it yourself
Day 4Explain: say why it works
  • 📚What Good Readers Do: one reading habit to try in the chapters
  • ✏️Daily Oral Language: fix a sentence, often from the book
  • 🌱Word study: a Latin root, a spelling pattern, and vocabulary from the chapters
  • 🧩Grammar: one sentence skill each week, practiced with sentences from the book
  • 🪶Poetry Corner: a short classic poem on Days 2 and 4
  • ✅Virtue Watch: courage, perseverance or justice, found in the chapter
  • 📖Read Like a Writer: one sentence from the book to notice and try
  • 🪨Stones in the River: the unit’s word game
  • 🖼️Real pictures: old maps, paintings and photos of the places in the story
  • 🎙️Fluency: a short passage read three times, then recorded

Poetry Corner

On Days 2 and 4, your student reads a short classic poem (Emily Dickinson and Robert Frost in Week 18) and learns to tell the speaker, the voice inside the poem, from the poet who wrote it. It’s the same idea as the novel: Isaac tells the story, but Tim Tingle wrote it. Reading the poems aloud together takes two minutes and helps a lot.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

This book is about a forced removal, and people die in it, including the narrator. It is written for young readers and never lingers, but it doesn’t look away either. It’s fine to say “that’s a hard part of history” and talk it through. Here is where the hardest parts fall, so nothing surprises you.

Week 18 · Chapters 1–7
Old people say goodbye to their land in painful ways. Men with torches burn the town at night; Isaac’s hair catches fire, and a man fires a shotgun at the family. Soldiers hand out blankets.
Week 19 · Chapters 8–11
Smallpox spreads through the swamp camp, and people Isaac knows die. On the walk, in freezing rain, Isaac’s feet are cut and bleeding. At the end of the week, five-year-old Nita dies in the cold.
Week 20 · Chapters 12–15
Nita’s mother carries her body as the walk goes on. Nita’s parents reveal that soldiers took their twelve-year-old daughter, Naomi, to cook for them, and knocked her father down when he tried to stop them.
Week 21 · Chapter 18
A wolf attacks and kills Isaac. It is told briefly but plainly, in his own voice. From here on, Isaac narrates as a ghost. It may help to read this chapter together or check in afterward.
Week 22 · The bonepickers
The book describes the bonepickers, who in Choctaw tradition cared for the bodies of the dead and cleaned their bones. Naomi hides in their wagon.
Weeks 23–24 · The ending
Soldiers threaten the Choctaw camps with shotguns, and the panther fights the wolf. The book ends with Naomi safe with her family.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Where in the book does it say that?” when your student answers a question.
  • Ask “Is that the story or the history?” It’s the question the whole unit builds toward.
  • Ask the questions in each weekly guide. Talking it through first makes the writing easier.
  • Be the audience for the fluency reading and the finished writing.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Fixing their drafts. Revising is a lesson of its own.
  • Telling them what happens next. Isaac promises a ghost story; let him tell it.
