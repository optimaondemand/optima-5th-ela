Daily Oral Language
Study the model, then fix the sentence

Sentence 1 — The Correct Model

This sentence is from the opening of Chapter I. Tap words you notice are correct. Watch for capitals, commas, and plural endings.

Silently they floated in on the town, but when their icy eyes sighted the first dead fish, first bits of garbage about the ships and wharves, they began to scream and quarrel.

Sentence 2 — Fix It

This sentence has mistakes. Rewrite it correctly in your notebook, using the model as a guide.

the bells rang out over fish street but no one in the crooked little house even stirred

Corrected:

The bells rang out over Fish Street, but no one in the crooked little house even stirred.

Compare with the model: both sentences start with a capital letter, name a real place with a capital ("Fish Street," like "wharves"), and use a comma before "but" to join two complete sentences.

🌱 Morphology: scrib / script
Latin · "to write"

This week you're becoming a historical writer yourself, so it fits perfectly that this week's root means "to write." Watch for scrib and script hiding inside all kinds of words.

📌 Examples

describe — to write or tell details about something

subscribe — to sign your name (write it) to agree to something

manuscript — a document written by hand

inscription — words written or carved onto something

🔤 Vocabulary
Words from Chapter I

Historical fiction is full of words we don't use every day. Good readers don't skip them — they decode them. Let's practice on the word apprentice.

📖 Step 1 — Read the Sentence

"As an apprentice he was little more than a slave until he had served his master seven years."

What clues does this sentence give you about what an apprentice is?

🧩 Step 2 — Look at the Word Parts

This word doesn't break apart into familiar parts — it's a whole vocabulary word to learn from context, the way Johnny learns his trade by watching and doing.

✅ Step 3 — Confirm the Definition

Apprentice: a young person legally bound to a master to learn a trade, receiving training instead of wages. Were you close? The sentence's mention of "served his master seven years" was your best clue.

✏️ Grammar: Dialogue Punctuation
Define the skill

Spoken words go inside quotation marks. A dialogue tag (like said Johnny) tells us who is speaking. A comma usually separates the spoken words from the tag.

👉 From the mentor text

‘All right,’ said Johnny blandly, ‘just so you’re up.’ Comma before the tag, tag stays lowercase, quotation marks open again for the rest of the speech.

Notice the tag "said Johnny blandly" sits in the middle of the sentence, with commas on both sides, and the quotation marks close and reopen around it.

🎯

Practice: Spot the Tag! Tap the word in each sentence that is the dialogue tag verb (the word that tells us how someone spoke).

‘Coming, ma’am,’ Dove turned over for one more snooze.
‘Here it is,’ piped Dusty pertly.
‘Gosh, Johnny. I’m sorry,’ snickered Dove.

🔠 Spelling: Historical Vocabulary Syllables
Discover the pattern

Don't look up the rule yet — look at the two groups of words below and see what you can figure out on your own.

🔍 Group A

ap•PREN•tice · co•LO•ni•al · mar•i•NER · his•TOR•i•cal

Count the syllables (word beats) in each word. What do you notice?

🔍 Group B

silver + smith · wharf + side · black + smith · store + house

These words are each made of two smaller words joined together. Can you name the two parts in each one?

The Pattern: Historical and academic vocabulary is often multisyllabic (3 or more word beats), like apprentice and colonial. Many trade words are also compound words — two smaller words joined together, like silversmith (silver + smith).
