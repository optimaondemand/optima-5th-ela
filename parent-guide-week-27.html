This Week at a Glance

The race ends. At Callao, in Peru, the Sea Raven buys up every lump of coal in town. Out of fuel a day from San Francisco, the Lady Wilma burns the lumber in her hold and pulls ahead to win at the Golden Gate. In San Francisco everything costs a fortune, and Jack and Praiseworthy have thirty-eight dollars. A shaggy miner named Quartz Jackson asks Praiseworthy for a haircut, and the gold dust in his whiskers gives them their start. Then it is up the river to Sacramento City and onto a stagecoach for the mines, where the chapter ends on a cliffhanger. On Day 4 your student reads the real words of Bayard Taylor, a reporter who landed in San Francisco in August 1849.

💛 A note for families: words from 1963
This book was written in 1963, and two chapters this week use that time’s unkind language. Chapter 8 calls Chinese people “silent” and their braids “pigtails,” writes a laundryman’s English as broken speech, and has a miner say “mighty white of you,” a racist expression. In Chapter 9 Jack fears “savages,” and a steward calls Native Californians “Digger Indians” who are “quite tame.” The lessons name this calmly in a short folded note before each chapter: the words were insults, and here is the true history. Chinese immigrants began arriving in 1849, ran laundries, mined and cooked, and faced unfair laws such as special taxes on foreign miners. The Sacramento Valley and foothills were the homelands of the Nisenan, Miwok and Maidu peoples, and the Gold Rush brought them terrible harm; historians estimate California’s Native population fell from about 150,000 in 1848 to about 30,000 by 1870. California’s Native nations are still here today. The lessons do not invent any voices for these communities.
Reading skill
Problem-solving and cause and effect. Your student tracks a chain: a problem, the fix a character chooses, and the new problem that fix causes.
Big idea
Generosity. Praiseworthy will not take money for the haircut: “Consider it a modest wedding present.”

New This Week

🚢 Race to the Golden Gate
The week’s word game. It is the last night of the race and the Lady Wilma has no coal. Each right answer (a -tion or -sion spelling, a run or pull root, a word family) throws a plank of lumber into her furnace and she surges ahead; a miss lets the Sea Raven creep up.
🌿 Virtue Watch: generosity
After reading, your student sorts everyday examples, picks the most generous moment in San Francisco, rates Praiseworthy’s gift of a horn spoon, and on Day 4 picks the most generous moment of the week.
🗺️ Real history, kept apart
A short card on Day 2: in 1849 crews left their ships for the gold fields, and hundreds of empty ships filled the cove. One of them, the Niantic, was hauled ashore, used as a store, and later had a hotel built on top of it. A piece of it was dug up in 1978 (National Park Service).
📖 Read Like a Writer, every day
One line from the book a day, chosen to show sequence and pacing, like “A deep throb ran through the ship — and then another. Then a splash of the sidewheels could be heard. Then another. And another.”

Day 1 · PlanDay 2 · DraftDay 3 · ReviseDay 4 · Submit

The adventure gets harder in the middle. Day 1: plan with three questions (what goes wrong, what the hero tries and how it makes things worse, and the moment of greatest pressure). Day 2: draft, with sentence frames for help. Day 3: revise with a checklist: steps in a clear order, one tense moment slowed down and ended with a short sentence, and an ending on a question, not an answer. Day 4: submit.

Poetry Corner: Pacing, Meter and Line Length

I sprang to the stirrup, and Joris, and he;
I galloped, Dirck galloped, we galloped all three;Robert Browning, “How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix” (1845)

Meter is the beat under the words, and Browning’s beat sounds like hooves: da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM. On Day 4, Robert Louis Stevenson’s “From a Railway Carriage” (1885) shows how line length sets the speed: long, packed lines, then short ones that flash past like a station seen from a train window.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. How did the Sea Raven’s coal end up helping the Lady Wilma win?Listen for: she piled so much coal on deck that she sat low and slow; the Lady Wilma burned her lumber and pulled ahead.
  2. Why did Quartz Jackson want every lock of hair saved?Listen for: his beard was full of gold dust from the diggings; Jack panned it out, and the gold was theirs.
  3. What did Bayard Taylor notice on his first day in San Francisco?Listen for: tents and canvas houses, goods piled outside, porters paid two dollars, newspapers sold at a dollar apiece, “complete bewilderment.”
  4. What happened to the ships whose crews ran off to the gold fields?Listen for: they sat empty in the cove; some became stores, hotels or a jail; some ended up buried under the city.
  5. What do curr/curs and tract mean? Name a word for each.Listen for: run (current, excursion); pull (extract, traction).
  6. Read me the most tense moment in your adventure. How did you slow it down?Listen for: small steps in order, then one short sentence.
