🌱 Morphology: Root rupt
Explain — why the prefix matters

Last day with rupt. Now explain what the prefixes are doing. Erupt, interrupt, disrupt and abrupt all describe a break — but a reader who knows the prefixes knows exactly which one. Match each word to the break it names.

📌 Quick Recap

rupt = break

erupt — to break out (e- = out)

interrupt — to break between (inter- = between)

disrupt — to break apart (dis- = apart)

abrupt — broken off short, with no warning (ab- = off)

✏️ Grammar: Proper Nouns, Titles & Historical Names
Explain the importance

Last day. Now explain why it matters. Capitalization is not politeness — it carries meaning. “Doctor Warren” is a particular man; “a doctor” is anyone. Getting it wrong quietly changes what a sentence says.

👉 Quick Recap

Specific names are capitalized: Paul Revere, Boston, Griffin’s Wharf, the Sons of Liberty

A title is capitalized when it sits in front of a name: Doctor Warren, Mr. Hancock — but “the doctor examined Johnny’s hand.”

General nouns stay lowercase: a silversmith, an apprentice, the harbor

Newspapers and books are italicized: the Boston Observer

🎯

Practice: Capital Spotter! One version of each pair is capitalized correctly. Tap it.

Which version says what the writer means? Johnny went to see a doctor about his hand. Johnny went to see Doctor Warren about his hand.
Which version says what the writer means? The boys joined the sons of liberty. The boys joined the Sons of Liberty.
Which version says what the writer means? Parliament passed the Tea Act in 1773. parliament passed the tea act in 1773.

🔠 Spelling: Suffix -ous
Utilize — the tricky ones

Last day with -ous. These are the ones that catch people out: the soft g and c words that keep their e, and the y words that turn to i. Sort them and say why.

📌 Quick Recap

-ous = full of. It turns a noun into an adjective.

Just add it: danger → dangerous, poison → poisonous

Drop the silent e: fame → famous, nerve → nervous

Keep the e after soft g or c: courage → courageous, outrage → outrageous — the e protects the soft sound

Change y to i: fury → furious, glory → glorious, vary → various

What Good Readers Do
Synthesise the week — setting as a force in the story

You have spent the week building background. Now put it together. In historical fiction the setting is not scenery — it is a force that acts on the characters. Merchant Lyte can accuse Johnny of theft and be believed, and the reason is not evidence. It is who each of them is in this particular city, in this particular year.

🔍 How to Do It
  1. Look back across all four chapters and list what the setting has decided for Johnny.
  2. Separate what happened to him from what the world did with it.
  3. Ask what a modern reader has to be told in order to understand the stakes.
