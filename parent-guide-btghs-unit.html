About the Book

By the Great Horn Spoon! by Sid Fleischman was first published in 1963. It is a comic adventure. In January 1849, twelve-year-old Jack Flagg and Praiseworthy, his family’s English butler, stow away on a ship from Boston bound around Cape Horn for California. They want to strike gold and save Jack’s Aunt Arabella from losing the family home. On the way, the calm, clever butler and the eager boy become true partners.

We read it because it is funny, fast and full of real Gold Rush detail: the long sea voyage, crowded San Francisco, the mining camps, and the prices miners paid. Each week your student also learns the skills good adventure writers use, and writes an adventure of their own.

“It was not once upon a time — it was precisely the twenty-seventh day of January in the year 1849.”Chapter 1

🧭

Story and History: What’s What

Made up
Jack, Praiseworthy, the ships Lady Wilma and Sea Raven, and the race between them are invented. So are the tall tales the miners tell about each other.
Real
The Gold Rush, the routes around Cape Horn and across Panama, San Francisco’s Long Wharf, Hangtown (now Placerville) and Sutter’s Mill are real. Day 1 of the unit teaches the history first, with real numbers from the Library of Congress, the National Park Service and others.

How a Week Works

Every week has four lessons, one fluency reading, and a Check-In. Each lesson follows the same path, so your student always knows what comes next. Across the four days, the grammar and word skills build:

Day 1Notice: work out the rule
Day 2Identify: find and sort
Day 3Use: build it yourself
Day 4Explain: say why it works
  • 📚What Good Readers Do: one reading habit to try in the chapters
  • ✏️Daily Oral Language: fix a sentence, often from the book
  • 🌱Word study: a Latin root, a spelling pattern, and vocabulary from the chapters
  • 🧩Grammar: one sentence skill each week, practiced with sentences from the book
  • 🪶Poetry Corner: a short classic poem on Days 2 and 4
  • ✅Virtue Watch: a virtue found in the chapter (loyalty in Week 25)
  • 📖Read Like a Writer: one sentence from the book to notice and try
  • 🎲A word game: a new one each week, tied to the chapters
  • 🖼️Real pictures: old maps, prints and paintings from the Gold Rush years
  • 🎙️Fluency: a short passage read three times, then recorded

How the Unit Ends

⛏️ Real history, kept apart (Week 30)
The novel’s last chapters pass through Coloma, where gold was found. A Day 1 card tells the real story: James Marshall’s discovery at Sutter’s Mill on January 24, 1848, a worker’s diary from that day, and the Nisenan valley of Cullumah. The story and the record stay in separate boxes, the way they have all unit.
🧭 Words from 1963
Two short passages in the last week use unkind words from the time the book was written: one about Chinese miners in Chapter 17 and one about Hawaiian sailors in Chapter 18. A folded note on that day’s Reading tab names each one plainly and gives the true history. The Week 30 guide has the details.
📘 End-of-novel project: From Boston to the Long Wharf
After Lesson 30.4, your student makes a four-page booklet about the whole book: the adventure’s shape from problem to ending, six turning points told fast or slow, one funny moment and one figure of speech, and the big idea. The last page holds 5 to 10 sentences from the adventure story your student published this unit. Every moment needs a chapter number. Your student taps Make my booklet, saves it as a PDF, and uploads it in Canvas. It is graded out of 20 points with a rubric on the page.

Your Student’s Writing

Writer’s Workshop
PlanDraftAdd CraftRevise & EditPublish

Across the six weeks, your student writes an adventure story of their own, inspired by Jack and Praiseworthy but completely their own. Week 25 is the plan: a hero, a problem, a setting, and what is at stake. Week 26 drafts a scene with dialogue, and Week 27 builds a middle that raises the stakes. In Week 28 the story rests while your student writes about the novel: a claim about its theme, proved with what the characters choose. Week 29 revises one paragraph for voice, and Week 30 adds a strong ending and publishes the finished, edited story. Each day also has a Read Like a Writer box with one of Fleischman’s sentences to study and imitate.

Poetry Corner

On Days 2 and 4, your student reads a short classic poem. This unit starts with humor and tone (Edward Lear’s “The Jumblies” and Lewis Carroll’s “The Walrus and the Carpenter” in Week 25), then moves through voice, rhythm and theme. Read the poems aloud together; they are meant to be heard.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

This is a funny book, and most of it is gentle. It was also written in 1963 about 1849, and a few parts show the attitudes of those times. The lessons don’t hide them. They name them plainly and calmly, and Day 1 of the unit tells the real history the book leaves out. It’s fine to pause and say, “That’s how people talked then, and here’s why it’s hurtful.”

Native Californians
The book uses words for Native people that were common then and hurtful now. In Chapter 1, Jack imagines “wild Indians” to fight off. In Chapters 9 and 15, the characters call California’s Native people “Digger Indians,” a name miners used as an insult, and treat them as curiosities. The book doesn’t mention what the Gold Rush cost them: about 150,000 Native Californians lived there in 1848, and twelve years later only about 30,000 were left. Their nations are still in California today.
Chinese and Mexican miners
In Chapter 8, a Chinese laundryman is drawn as a type, with a pigtail and broken English, and in another place a character says “mighty white of you” to mean kind. Chinese, Mexican and Chilean miners were a large part of the real Gold Rush, and many faced unfair taxes and violence.
Rough-and-tumble action
A holdup by masked robbers, a near-hanging played for comedy in a mining camp, a grizzly bear, a bare-knuckle boxing match, and a steamboat explosion. No one the reader cares about is badly hurt.
Talk about money
The partners are trying to save a family home. The book is honest that most miners found little gold and that prices in the gold fields were sky-high.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Where in the book does it say that?” when your student answers a question.
  • Ask “Is that the story or the history?” when the novel mentions something real.
  • Read a funny scene aloud together and let your student do the voices.
  • Be the audience for the fluency reading and the finished adventure story.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Fixing their story drafts. Revising is a lesson of its own.
  • Telling them how the book ends.
