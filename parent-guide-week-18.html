This Week at a Glance

A new novel begins. Isaac is a ten-year-old Choctaw boy in Mississippi around 1830, and he tells the reader on the very first page that he is a ghost. “Treaty Talk” comes to his town: the United States wants the Choctaw Nation’s land. Over these seven chapters the old people say goodbye to the river, men on horseback burn the town at night, and Isaac’s family hides on an island in a swamp with about a hundred other Choctaws. Then, in Chapter 7, comes a snowball fight. In Writer’s Workshop, your student writes a true moment from their own life, told from the inside with “I,” the way Isaac tells his.

💛 A heads-up for families
This book tells the story of the Choctaw removal, when the United States forced the Choctaw people to leave their homeland in Mississippi after the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek (1830). Isaac tells us from the start that he does not survive the story. Some parts this week are hard. In Chapters 2 and 3, the oldest people in town say goodbye to their land in painful ways: an old man rubs his back against tree bark until it bleeds, and old women jump into the river and dance on its sharp stones. Isaac sometimes sees things before they happen, and in these visions he sees who is going to die. In Chapter 4, men with torches burn the town at night. Isaac’s hair catches fire and he puts it out with his blanket, and a man fires a shotgun at the family. Isaac’s father throws himself over the family, and a neighbor is hurt in the shoulder. In Chapter 6, soldiers hand out blankets, and Isaac’s mother keeps her family away from them. The book uses a Choctaw word, Nahullo, for the white men who come. Reading together, or checking in after each chapter, helps. The unit guide has the history and what comes later.
Reading skill
Two things at once. Your student builds the history behind the story (the Choctaw Nation, Treaty Talk, the move) while listening to Isaac’s voice: what he says plainly, what he won’t explain, and what he warns you about before it happens.
Big idea
A first-person narrator shows what a history book can’t. We see the removal through one boy’s eyes: his family, his dog Jumper, his fear, and the moments that are still funny.

“I am a ghost. I am not a ghost when this book begins, so you have to pay very close attention.”Isaac, Chapter 1

Words to Know

Use them at home!
treatya formal written agreement between two nations
Nahulloa white person; the Choctaw word Isaac’s family uses for the men who come on horseback
missionariespeople sent to a place to teach their religion
scatteredspread out over an area rather than standing together
torchesburning sticks carried as a light or a weapon
blowgunslong tubes used to shoot darts; Isaac and Luke make their own
smotheringcovering something so closely that it cannot breathe or burn
Word parts
mem / memor means “memory.” script means “write.” Isaac is doing both: remembering, and writing it down.memoir · memorial · memorize · manuscript · inscription · description
Spelling
Open and closed syllables. A syllable that ends on a vowel is open, and the vowel says its name. One that ends on a consonant is closed.open: na·tion, ce·dar, fro·zen · closed: blan·ket, gov·ern·ment
Grammar
Appositives rename the noun beside them and sit between commas. This week also asks for precise nouns instead of vague ones.Jumper, Isaac’s dog, followed him everywhere.

New in the Lessons

Starting with this book, the lessons have a few new pieces. Look for these:

🎧 A narrator for every tab
A short clip at the top of each tab says what to do and what to watch for. The narrator never speaks for Isaac or any Choctaw character; the book’s own words are for your student to read.
🪨 Stones in the River
The week’s word game. In Chapter 3, Isaac remembers a river with sharp stones on the bottom. In the game, the stones help: each holds a word from Isaac’s world, and your student splits it into syllables (and later finds its root) to cross.
🖼️ Real pictures from the time and place
Each Reading tab has one: a map of Mississippi printed around 1831 with the Choctaw Nation marked, photos of cypress swamps like the one where Isaac’s family hides, and George Catlin’s 1834–35 painting of a Choctaw stickball game.
🗣️ Choctaw words in the book
Isaac tells his story in English, but some Choctaw words stay in it, like Hoke (“okay”). A card on Day 1 explains the ones your student meets this week.
🧭 “What to do” lines
Each activity now opens with a short list: what to do, why it matters, and where your student will use it. If an answer is off, a hint points back to the rule instead of just saying “try again.”

Your Student’s Writing: A Moment From the Inside

Writer’s Workshop
Day 1 · PlanDay 2 · DraftDay 3 · ReviseDay 4 · Submit

One short piece, start to finish this week. Day 1: list 3–4 moments from your own life when you felt fear, wonder or loss. Day 2: pick one and write 3–5 sentences about it from the inside, with “I.” Day 3: add one inside detail, something you saw, heard, felt or thought. Day 4: write a clean final version of 5–7 sentences with a specific opening, at least one inside detail and a strong closing line, plus one sentence on what Isaac taught you about telling a story from the inside.

Poetry Corner: Who Is Talking?

Nature’s first green is gold,
Her hardest hue to hold.
Her early leaf’s a flower;
But only so an hour.
Then leaf subsides to leaf.
So Eden sank to grief,
So dawn goes down to day.
Nothing gold can stay.Robert Frost, “Nothing Gold Can Stay” (1923), Day 4. On Day 2: Emily Dickinson, “We grow accustomed to the Dark” (about 1862).

This week’s poems teach the speaker: the voice talking inside a poem, who is not the same as the poet. It’s the same idea as the novel. Isaac tells the story, but Tim Tingle wrote it.

How to help: Read the poem aloud together. Ask who the speaker is and how they feel about the gold that can’t stay. Then ask what the “gold” was that Isaac’s family lost.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Isaac says he is a ghost on the very first page. Why tell the reader that?Listen for: so you pay close attention. He also says he sees things before they happen, and that Treaty Talk is why he became a ghost.
  2. What did the old people do down by the river?Listen for: they were saying goodbye to their home. Mister Jonah rubbed his back against a tree until it bled, and the old women jumped from the pier and danced on the sharp stones in the river.
  3. What did Isaac’s father do the night the town burned?Listen for: when a man aimed a shotgun at the family, he threw himself over them. Then he told them to run and stay together, and they hid in the woods.
  4. When the soldiers brought blankets, what did Isaac’s mother do?Listen for: she held her family back. “We do not need their blankets.” Isaac doesn’t know why yet.
  5. Why is Chapter 7 funny?Listen for: Luke called Isaac a snow monster, and Isaac’s snowball missed Luke and hit their father in the face.
  6. Read me your moment. Which line is your inside detail?Listen for: a line about something they saw, heard, felt or thought in that moment, told with “I.”
