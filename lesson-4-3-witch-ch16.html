✏️ Grammar: Dialogue Punctuation
Use — fix the punctuation yourself
📖

Remember the Rule: When writing dialogue, put quotation marks around the speaker's exact words. Use a comma to separate the dialogue tag (she said) from the quote. Start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes. End punctuation goes inside the closing quotation mark.

Now use the rules. Chapter 16 is almost all conversation — Nat in the stocks, Hannah at the fire, John making his choice. Below, one version of each exchange is punctuated correctly. Find it, and notice exactly what the other version got wrong.

👉 From the novel

Comma before the tag: “Thanksgiving is cancelled,” Matthew said.

Tag first, comma after it: Nat said, “It was well worth it.”

Question mark stays inside: “Where is the charter?” the governor demanded.

New speaker, new paragraph: every time the speaker changes, start a new line.

🔠 Spelling: Suffix -ion / -tion / -sion
Apply — build nouns from base verbs
📖

The Rule: The suffixes -ion, -tion, and -sion turn verbs into nouns meaning "the act or result of." Compare: create → creation, discuss → discussion, decide → decision. The base word often changes spelling: drop a silent e (create → creation), change a d to s (decide → decision), or double a consonant (admit → admission).

Every multi-syllable word has one syllable that gets more emphasis — the stressed syllable. Knowing which syllable is stressed helps you spell tricky words, because the unstressed syllables often have unclear vowel sounds.

📌 Quick Reminder

PUN-ish-ment — stress on the first syllable

re-PENT-ance — stress on the second syllable

con-se-QUENCE — stress on the third syllable

The stressed syllable is louder and clearer. Unstressed syllables are quieter — their vowels can sound mushy.

What Good Readers Do
Track theme through consequences

Good readers notice when an author develops theme through consequences. In Chapter 16, multiple characters act on their beliefs — and each one faces a cost. Nat pranks William and gets banished. John enlists and breaks with his mentor. Kit keeps visiting Hannah despite the risk. Track what happens when characters stand up for what they believe.

🔍 How to Do It
  1. Identify the narrator's view: Ask what the narrator knows or reveals that the characters may not — what information does the reader get that shapes how we see events?
  2. Notice character perspectives: Pay attention to how different characters interpret the same event — their beliefs, fears, and backgrounds shape what they see.
  3. Compare what's known: Ask how the narrator's information and each character's perspective create tension, irony, or sympathy in the story.
🧠 As you read, ask yourself

What does each character sacrifice for what they believe in? Is the cost worth it?

How does Hannah’s wisdom — “thee has never escaped at all if love is not there” — connect to the choices other characters are making?

Strengthen your paragraph with the Revision Checklist

🎯

Today’s focus: Revise your perspective paragraph using the checklist below. Strong revision means checking that every part of your paragraph is doing its job — and making it stronger where it isn’t.

👀 Watch How — Meet Alex, our example writer

Alex reads the draft and uses the checklist to find what needs strengthening:

BEFORE (draft)
In Chapter 14, the author develops Kit’s perspective by showing a quiet moment that changes how Kit sees her uncle. When Kit watches Matthew hold a handful of soil “with a curious reverence,” she begins to understand that the land means something deeper to him. This reveals that Kit is starting to see Connecticut through her uncle’s eyes.
AFTER (revised)
In Chapter 14, the author develops Kit’s perspective by showing a quiet moment that shifts how she sees her new home. When Kit watches Matthew hold a handful of Connecticut soil “with a curious reverence,” she realizes the land means freedom to him — something she has never considered. Until this moment, Kit has seen Connecticut only as a cold, unwelcoming place. This scene reveals that Kit is beginning to understand the Puritan world on its own terms, not just through the lens of her Barbados upbringing.

What changed: Alex added a second layer of analysis (“something she has never considered”), included a contrast (“Until this moment…”), and connected the evidence to the bigger picture of Kit’s perspective shift.

📋 Today’s Tool: Revision Checklist

Read your draft, then check each item. If something is missing, revise before submitting.

🪶 Revision Sentence Starters
  • To add deeper analysis: “This is significant because…”
  • To add contrast: “Until this moment, [character] had always…”
  • To connect to the bigger picture: “This shift in perspective matters because…”
  • To strengthen a quote introduction: “The narrator reveals this when [character]…”
