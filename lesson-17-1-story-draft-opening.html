1
🌱 Morphology: narr / fic
Latin · “to tell” / “to make or form”

This is the perfect week to meet these two roots: narr means “to tell,” and fic means “to make or form.” A story is something you narrate (tell) that is also fiction (made or formed from imagination).

📌 Examples

narrate — to tell a story or give an account of events

narrator — the person telling the story (-or changes the verb to a noun for "one who does")

fiction — writing about imagined events and characters (-ion changes the root to a noun)

fictional — made up, not real (-al changes the noun to an adjective)

3
✏️ Grammar: Complex Sentences
Learn: define cause-and-effect complex sentences

A complex sentence joins one independent clause (a complete thought) with one dependent clause, using a subordinating conjunction like because, since, until, although, or so that. In historical fiction, complex sentences are perfect for showing cause and effect — how one event in history led to another.

👉 From the mentor model

“Nathaniel pressed his back against the cold brick wall, listening for footsteps, and gripped the folded letter inside his coat until his knuckles ached.”

The word until joins the idea of gripping the letter to its result — his knuckles aching. That's cause and effect built right into one sentence.

🎯

Practice: Spot the Subordinating Conjunction! Tap the word in each sentence that joins the two ideas and shows cause and effect.

Nathaniel because promised he walked the letter across town before sunrise, he had made a promise.
Sarah dropped her books and when quickly she heard the bells ringing across the city.
The apprentice worked late into the night so that tired shop the order would be ready by morning.
4
🔠 Spelling: Suffix Changes in Narrative Verbs
Discover: -ed and -ing spelling changes

Look at these two groups of narrative verbs. What happens to each base word when you add -ing?

📌 Group A

hope → hoping — wait, that doesn't look right. Try again: hope → hoping? Notice the silent e is dropped: hoping becomes hoping. write → writing. describe → describing.

📌 Group B

run → running. stop → stopping. drop → dropping. Notice the final consonant doubles.

🤔

Discovery questions: Look at Group A — what happens to the silent e before you add -ing? Now look at Group B — what happens to the last letter? Can you figure out when each rule applies?

The rule: If a verb ends in a silent e (hope, write, describe), drop the e before adding -ing or -ed (hoping/hoped, writing/no change for irregular wrote, describing/described). If a short verb ends in one vowel + one consonant (run, stop, drop), double the final consonant before adding -ing or -ed (running/no -ed for irregular ran, stopping/stopped, dropping/dropped).

Open Your Story Map
Plan before you draft

Think back to Chapter I of Johnny Tremain, which opened with gulls waking over Boston harbor and ordinary morning chores — not a battle, not a speech, just daily life that made 1773 Boston feel real. Good historical fiction almost always starts small and sensory before it gets big.

🪶
Ms. Birchum's Example
A teacher-written mentor model — not from a published book
Read the opening below, then plan your own story.
📌 The mentor model opening

“The wind off the harbor carried the sharp smell of salt and gunpowder smoke that still lingered three days after the battle. Nathaniel pressed his back against the cold brick wall, listening for footsteps, and gripped the folded letter inside his coat until his knuckles ached. He had promised to deliver it before sunrise, no matter what waited in the dark streets between him and the print shop.”

Notice the sensory detail (smell of salt and gunpowder), the real historical anchor (three days after a battle), and the character's want (deliver the letter) and conflict (danger in the dark streets). That's exactly what your story map below will help you plan.

🗺️ Setting

Choose a time and place: Revolutionary Boston (extending Johnny Tremain's world) or another well-documented historical moment you're curious about.

📜 Real Historical Event

What real, documented event will anchor your story? (A battle, a law, a journey, an invention, an announcement.)

🪶 Main Character

Invent a young person living through this moment. What do they want more than anything?

⚔️ Conflict

What stands in their way — both outside them (an external conflict) and inside them (an internal conflict)?

⏸️ Before You Draft

Jot your setting, event, character, and conflict in your journal using the story map above.

⏸️ Before You Draft

Think of one sensory detail (something you could see, hear, or smell) that would make your opening moment feel real.
