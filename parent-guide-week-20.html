This Week at a Glance

Nita’s ghost comes to Isaac with a request: find her sister. That night around the campfire, two secrets come out. Gabe tells everyone that soldiers took his older daughter, Naomi, and Isaac tells his family that he will soon be a ghost. Before morning, Joseph, the grandson of Old Man and Old Woman, wakes Isaac. He knows where Naomi is. Joseph tells how he was caught by the soldiers and how he got away. Then the ghost of Old Man gives Isaac a plan: go to the Choctaw Council wagon and help the councilmen, and watch for Naomi. In Writer’s Workshop, your student writes a theme paragraph.

💛 A heads-up for families
In Chapter 12, Nita’s parents carry her body on the walk, and Isaac has a frightening daydream about an icicle falling on him. In Chapter 13, Gabe tells how a soldier took Naomi, knocked him down with a shotgun, and threatened to take Nita too. In Chapter 14, Joseph tells how soldiers left him bleeding in the snow, tied him up, and laughed as they told him the blankets had killed his town. He escapes by turning into a panther; Isaac had heard stories of panther people but never believed them. The lessons name these moments plainly and do not dwell on them.
Reading skill
How a story’s conflict rises. Your student names who wants what and what stands in the way, follows how each event makes the next one possible, and finds where the danger changes from the road to the soldiers themselves.
Big idea
Telling the truth can take courage. Gabe and Isaac each kept a secret to protect the people they love. Once the secrets are told, the family can act together.

Words to Know

Use them at home!
determinedhaving your mind firmly made up, no matter how hard the thing is
treatya formal agreement between governments; in the story, the one that forced the Choctaws from their homeland
councila group chosen to make decisions for their people
invisibleimpossible to see
permissionbeing allowed to do something by the person in charge
Word parts
path means “feeling, suffering.” sens/sent means “feel, perceive.” Sympathy is feeling for someone; empathy is feeling with them.sympathy · empathy · apathy · sensitive · sensation · sentiment
Spelling
Accent shifts. When a word grows, its strong beat can move. A related word helps you hear a vowel that is hard to hear.atom → atomic · major → majority · define → definite · history → historic
Grammar
Commas with clauses and transitions. A comma goes after an opening clause, and before and, but or so when they join two sentences. A comma alone can’t join two sentences.By nightfall the snow stopped, but the air turned bitter and cold.

New This Week

🔥 Council Fire
The week’s word game. Each log holds a pair of words, like courage and courageous. Your student says both out loud and taps the strong beat in each, to hear where it moves. Five pairs a day.
⚖️ Virtue Watch: justice
After reading, your student sorts moments, keeps a log, rates a moment on a meter, and on Day 4 picks the strongest moment of justice in the week.
🖼️ Paintings from the 1800s
Each lesson opens with a painting: a snowstorm in the Sierra Nevada, a moonlit winter night, moonlight in a winter forest, and Winslow Homer’s campfire. They were painted far from Mississippi, and the captions say so.
🗣️ Choctaw words
A card on Day 4 explains Halito (hello), Yakoke (thank you), Hoke (okay) and Pashofa (corn soup). The narrator never says Choctaw words aloud.

Your Student’s Writing: A Theme Paragraph

Writer’s Workshop
Day 1 · PlanDay 2 · DraftDay 3 · ReviseDay 4 · Submit

One paragraph that explains a theme from the novel and proves it with evidence. Day 1: pick a theme (survival, courage, justice, loss or memory) and note two pieces of evidence from the last few chapters. Day 2: write the first 3–4 sentences: a topic sentence that names the theme, then one piece of evidence. Day 3: check that each piece of evidence proves the theme, then add a second piece and a sentence that explains how both support it. Day 4: write the full paragraph in 5–7 sentences, ending with a closing sentence that returns to the theme in a new way.

Every day also has a Read Like a Writer box with one of Isaac’s sentences. This week they show how a sentence controls time: it can make the reader wait, as in “The icicle responds. It shakes, but it keeps everybody waiting,” or fit a whole night into a few words.

Poetry Corner: Line Breaks

Two roads diverged in a wood, and I—
I took the one less traveled by,
And that has made all the difference.Robert Frost, “The Road Not Taken” (1916), last lines, Day 2. On Day 4: Emily Dickinson, “A narrow fellow in the grass” (published 1866).

A poet chooses where each line ends. Frost breaks the line right after “and I—”, so the reader stops for a breath before the choice is made, just as the speaker did. Dickinson’s short lines move like the snake in her poem: there, then gone.

How to help: Read the Frost lines aloud together, pausing at the dash. Ask what that pause makes you feel, and when Isaac had to choose a road this week.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What did Nita’s ghost ask Isaac to do?Listen for: find her sister, to make her mother and father happy again. Nita told him to ask her father about it.
  2. Why did Gabe and Ruth keep Naomi a secret?Listen for: the soldiers said they would take Nita too if they told anyone. Now that Nita is gone, they can’t be hurt that way.
  3. What secret did Isaac tell his family, and why had he waited?Listen for: that he will soon be a ghost. He didn’t want to upset anyone after they had lost their home.
  4. How did Joseph get away from the soldiers?Listen for: he earned their trust by fishing for them, hid a sharp stone, cut the ropes at night and ran. When they found his cave, he turned into a panther and scared them off.
  5. What plan did Old Man’s ghost give Isaac?Listen for: go alone to the Choctaw Council wagon, help the councilmen, and watch for Naomi. Joseph should stay hidden in the woods.
  6. Read me your theme paragraph. What is the theme, and what proves it?Listen for: a topic sentence that names the theme, and two pieces of evidence from the book.
