🌱 Morphology: migr / mov / mot
Explain: why these roots matter

All week you've used the roots migr, mov, and mot — all meaning "to move" — to understand words like removal, migrate, motion, and movement. Learning a root like this doesn't just help with one word. It gives you a tool you can use for the rest of your life: any time you meet an unfamiliar word with migr, mov, or mot inside it, you already have a clue about what it means.

📌 Why This Matters

Historians and writers use words built from these roots constantly — immigrant, emotion, removal, promote. Knowing the root "to move" helps you figure out new words in any subject, not just history.

✏️ Grammar: Sentence Variety
Explain: why writers vary their sentences

This week you learned to notice, spot, and write with sentence variety — mixing short and long sentences and varying how sentences begin. But why do writers bother with this?

👉 Compare these two sentences

"Both of these ideas are part of the historical record." Short. Direct. Makes the reader pause on an important idea.

"President Andrew Jackson supported the Indian Removal Act of 1830, a law that allowed the government to make treaties exchanging Native homelands in the Southeast for land west of the Mississippi River." Long. Packed with detail. Explains a complicated idea fully.

🧠

Talk about it: If a writer used only short sentences, the writing might feel choppy. If a writer used only long sentences, a reader might get lost. Varying sentence length lets a writer slow the reader down for an important idea, then speed back up to explain more.

Research Passage: Primary & Secondary Sources
Read the passage below.
📜 Research Passage

Historians divide evidence into two categories. A primary source is a firsthand piece of evidence created at the actual time of an event — an original document, a letter, a photograph, or a treaty. A secondary source is something created later that describes, explains, or interprets an event — like a history book, an article, or a historian's summary.

The actual treaty document signed at Dancing Rabbit Creek on September 27, 1830, is a primary source. It contains the treaty's real terms, dates, and signatures from that exact moment in history — nothing added, nothing explained, just the original record.

Here is an example of a secondary source, written by a historian today: "Historians describe the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, signed in 1830, as the first removal treaty carried out under the Indian Removal Act. It explains, in modern language, how the treaty affected thousands of Choctaw families in the years that followed."

Both sources are valuable, but in different ways. The primary source shows you the exact original record, unchanged. The secondary source helps explain what that record meant and connects it to the bigger picture of history. Historians almost always use both kinds of sources together.
