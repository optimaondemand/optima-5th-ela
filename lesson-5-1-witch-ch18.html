What Good Readers Do
Set your reading lens

Good readers watch a theme develop across chapters. A theme is never announced; it is built. The author returns to the same big idea again and again, in different situations, until the pattern becomes visible.

Watch one idea in particular from here to the end of the book: what freedom actually costs. In Chapter 18 Kit wakes grateful and resolved, and hours later she is locked in the constable’s shed — the first locked door of her life. Ask yourself:

🔍 How to Do It
  1. Find the repeated idea: Notice which big ideas keep coming back — in different chapters, through different characters, in situations that look nothing alike.
  2. Track how it deepens: Ask what each new appearance adds. A theme develops; it does not simply repeat.
  3. Say it as a sentence: State the theme as a full sentence about people, not a single word. “Freedom” is a topic; “freedom is worth nothing if you have to become someone else to keep it” is a theme.

🌱 Morphology: Root struct
Define · struct = build

William’s house is going up all through these chapters — sixteen diamond-paned windows, the finest lot in Wethersfield. Meanwhile the town tries to take Kit apart. Both are the same Latin root at work: struct means build, and the prefix in front of it decides whether something is being built up, built into, or torn down.

📌 The Root

struct = build

construct — to build something together (con- = with)

destruction — the un-building of something (de- = down, away)

instruct — to build knowledge into someone (in- = into)

obstruct — to build something in the way (ob- = against)

Once you know struct, a long word stops being frightening. Reconstruction is just re- (again) + struct (build) + -ion (the thing). Building, again, the thing. That is the whole word.

🔤 Vocabulary
Decode & Discover — words from Chapter 18

Chapter 18 shifts from warmth and gratitude to terror. Kit’s world changes in a single knock on the door. Use the three-step strategy: context, word parts, then confirm.

🔍 3-Step Decode: chagrined

Step 1 — Context: “Kit was chagrined to realize how much she had taken for granted.” Kit feels embarrassed and disappointed in herself — the word describes a humbling realization.

Step 2 — Word Parts: Chagrin (a French-origin root meaning distress or vexation) + -ed (past tense / adjective). Together: feeling distressed by embarrassment or humiliation.

Step 3 — Confirm: Chagrined means feeling embarrassed or annoyed because of failure or disappointment. Kit is chagrined that she didn’t appreciate the family’s care sooner.

✏️ Grammar: Commas with Introductory Words & Phrases
Define — when do introductory elements need a comma?

When a sentence begins with an introductory word, phrase, or clause before the main subject and verb, you set it off with a comma. This comma tells the reader: “Pause here — the main idea is coming next.”

👉 Three patterns for introductory commas

Pattern 1 — Introductory word: “Suddenly, the constable appeared at the door.” A single word like suddenly, however, meanwhile, finally gets a comma.

Pattern 2 — Introductory phrase: “After the long night, Kit leaned against the settle.” A prepositional or participial phrase at the start gets a comma.

Pattern 3 — Introductory clause: “When the door opened, Kit saw the constable standing behind it.” A dependent clause before the main clause gets a comma.

🔍 How to find one — two questions

1. Where does the main sentence start? Find who or what the sentence is really about, and the verb that goes with it. In “Inside the dark shed, Kit huddled against the wall,” the main sentence is Kit huddled.

2. Is anything sitting in front of it? If yes, that is your introductory element, and the comma goes right where it ends — just before the main subject. If no, the sentence starts with its own subject and needs no comma at all.

That second question is the one people forget. Not every sentence has an introductory element, and a comma dropped in front of a plain subject is an error, not a courtesy.

🔠 Spelling: Suffix -ity & Abstract Nouns
Define — naming a quality

These chapters are full of things you cannot point at. Kit is accused out of cruelty. Prudence saves her out of loyalty. Speare needs nouns for qualities, and English builds them with -ity and its shorter partner -ty: take an adjective, add the ending, and you have named the quality itself. The meaning is simple. Choosing between the two endings — and knowing what each one does to the adjective — is the part worth learning.

📌 Building an Abstract Noun

Start with an adjective — a describing word. Add the suffix. You now have a noun naming the quality of being that thing.

cruel (adjective) → cruelty (noun)  ·  pure → purity

These are abstract nouns. You cannot point at cruelty or hold loyalty in your hand, but Chapter 18 is made of both.

Both endings sound the same out loud — “tee.” The card beside this one shows how to tell which one you are spelling.

⚖️ -ty or -ity?

-ty attaches to a base that is already finished. Nothing is removed: cruel → cruelty, loyal → loyalty, safe → safety.

-ity begins with a vowel, so it pushes the base around: pure → purity, secure → security, able → ability.

That is why safe keeps its e but scarce loses it. Safety takes -ty; scarcity takes -ity, and the e has to get out of the way.

Both endings name a quality. Only one of them disturbs the base. Decide which suffix you are adding first, and the spelling follows.

-ty leaves the base alone. cruel → cruelty, loyal → loyalty, safe → safety, honest → honesty. Write the adjective, then write ty.

-ity starts with a vowel, so a silent e has to go. pure → purity, secure → security, scarce → scarcity. The e has no job once a vowel arrives behind it.

A few bases reshape further before -ity. able → ability and possible → possibility turn -le into -il-; curious → curiosity and generous → generosity turn -ous into -os-. Say the noun aloud — the spelling follows the new sound.
