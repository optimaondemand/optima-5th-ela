The last week of the year: a real letter to next year’s fifth graders
4 lessons 1 recording Year-end project Check-In at week’s end

🧭 How the Lesson Pages Work

  • 🗂️Four tabs, in order: Warm-Up → Word Study → the day’s main work (Reflect, Fluency, Revise or Synthesize) → Assignment. A button at the bottom of each tab moves to the next.
  • 👣One step at a time. Each tab shows one activity at a time, and a Next button moves through its steps.
  • 📝Every answer goes on the page. There’s a box for each one.
  • 💾Answers save as you type, but only in that browser on that computer. Switching computers or clearing the browser loses them.
  • 📓Copy your work at the bottom of the Assignment tab, then paste it into the assignment in Canvas and submit. A lesson isn’t turned in until it’s submitted there.
  • 🎙️Fluency is a separate page in the Canvas module, because recording doesn’t work inside a lesson.
🧭

This Week at a Glance

There is no new chapter this week. Your student finishes the year’s Reader-Writer Portfolio by writing a real letter to next year’s fifth graders: which of this year’s five novels should they read first, and why? The letter comes with a Reader’s Portrait (who your student was as a reader in September, and who they are now), five Book Postcards, a Word Gift of three roots, a P.S., and an envelope your student designs. On Day 4 they record the letter and one poem from the year.

💛 A note for families: real letters and real books
Every book quote on the pages this week was checked against the novels. On Day 2 a short card shows a real letter from the Gold Rush: Louise Clappe, who signed her letters “Dame Shirley,” wrote 23 letters to her sister from California mining camps in 1851 and 1852. The card quotes only her goodbye to the camp. The year in review on Day 4 names How I Became a Ghost plainly: the story of a Choctaw boy on his people’s forced journey west. Your student’s letter is real too; teachers can pass it on to next year’s class.
Reading skill
Pulling the year together. Your student sets a purpose across five books, gathers evidence from two of them, rereads one passage for the author’s choices, and names an idea that runs through the whole year.
Big idea
Wisdom. Knowing what matters and making good choices with what you know, like Nat Bowditch, who checks his figures and lets the land prove him right.

New This Week

🗺️ The Year’s Map
The last word game of the year. A map shows five stops, one for each novel’s world, from Wethersfield to San Francisco. Each clue asks for a word built from a root your student learned while reading that book, and a right answer lights the stop.
🌿 Virtue Watch: wisdom
Your student sorts a letter writer’s choices, picks the wisest moment from the year’s books, rates Nat Bowditch’s quiet confidence, and chooses the piece of wisdom they would pass on.
✉️ Letters that crossed time
A Day 2 card shows how one real letter, written for one reader in 1852, has been read for more than 170 years.
📖 Read Like a Writer: voice, tone and pacing
Four real lines from the year’s novels: three short sentences that slow down (By the Great Horn Spoon!), a voice in three words (“We’re partners, sir”), a calm tone (Carry On, Mr. Bowditch), and a long list with a short ending (Johnny Tremain).

Your Student’s Writing: The Letter

Part 2 of the year-end portfolio
Day 1 · PlanDay 2 · DraftDay 3 · ReviseDay 4 · Seal & Send

The letter goes: Dear future 5th grader, then the Reader’s Portrait, a claim (“Read ___ before ___”), two reasons with a moment from both books, one fair point for the other book, a closing and a signature. The Word Gift and the P.S. come after. Students write to a ten-year-old who hasn’t read either book, so the letter never tells how a book ends.

🪶

Poetry Corner: A Poem Read Aloud

Greatly shining,
The Autumn moon floats in the thin sky;Amy Lowell, “Wind and Silver” (1925), first two lines

On Day 2 your student reads Lowell’s short poem three ways (flat, too fast, and the way it asks to be read) and explains what changed. On Day 4 they choose any poem from the year and read it the way it asks to be read, and that poem goes into the final recording.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Which book would you hand a new fifth grader first? Why?Listen for: two novels, and a reason with a moment from each.
  2. What kind of reader were you in September? What changed?Listen for: something specific, tied to a book.
  3. Read me your favorite line from your letter.Listen for: pauses in the right places and a warm, sure tone.
  4. What’s one root you would teach a younger kid?Listen for: the root, its meaning and a word built from it.
  5. Which poem did you choose to record, and why?Listen for: a poem from the year and one thing about how it sounds.

What Gets Turned In

  • Each lesson: the day’s work and that day’s part of the letter, copied and pasted into Canvas
  • Day 4: the envelope and the final letter
  • Recording: the letter and one poem, in the Canvas fluency assignment
  • Project: the Reader-Writer Portfolio booklet, one PDF
  • Check-In: at the end of the week

Done well looks like: a clear claim, two reasons with evidence from both books, exact quotes with chapters, a voice that sounds like your student, and a recording with pauses where they help.
